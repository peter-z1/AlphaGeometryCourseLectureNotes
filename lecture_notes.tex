\begin{abstract}
These notes grew out of a Fall 2025 special-topics course at Brown University
on Euclidean geometry and artificial intelligence.  They develop two subjects
in parallel: classical geometric reasoning and the construction of a small,
inspectable AlphaGeometry-style prover.  The cumulative programming project
develops a symbolic solver, a synthetic-data pipeline, a trainable language
model, and tests.  Those software and data artifacts are maintained separately
from this lecture-notes repository.  The aim is not to claim a
full reproduction of DeepMind's system, but to make the central neuro-symbolic
idea reproducible at undergraduate scale: a learned model proposes an
auxiliary construction, and a symbolic engine must check it and produce a
step-by-step proof.
\end{abstract}

\part{Foundations: geometry, deduction, and learned search}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec: Intro}
The course has two connected aims.  The first is mathematical: students learn
a broad selection of Euclidean geometry, including triangle and circle
geometry, classical constructions, incidence and projective configurations,
and a short introduction to Hilbert's axiomatic viewpoint
\cite{hartshorne2013geometry}. Power of a point, radical axes, Menelaus,
Ceva, Pappus, Pascal, and Brianchon are important examples in the notes, but
they are not an exhaustive list of the mathematics covered. The second aim is
computational: students learn to formalize a geometry problem, represent a
proof state, implement deductive and algebraic reasoning, generate synthetic
examples, and train a model to propose useful auxiliary points.

The original course met three times each week.  Two meetings emphasized
geometry and one emphasized implementation.  The two strands should not be
treated as unrelated: every software representation makes a mathematical
choice, and every theorem suggests operations that a solver may need.  For
example, choosing directed line angles modulo $\pi$ makes angle chasing linear
and fast, but it also discards orientation information that must sometimes be
stored separately.

Throughout the notes, a \keyterm{proof certificate} means a checkable record
of a symbolic proof. Each derived statement names the permitted theorem rule
or algebraic combination used and points to the earlier statements on which it
depends. A small independent checker can replay these steps from the givens to
the goal. We call a consequence \keyterm{certified} when the solver has this
replayable justification for it. Numerical agreement in one diagram and a
language model's confidence are not certificates.

\begin{lecturebox}{Intended audience and prerequisites}
The notes are written for undergraduates who know elementary Euclidean
geometry. Some familiarity with programming is helpful, but no substantial
software-engineering experience is assumed. Before the first coding milestone
we introduce the small subset of Python used repeatedly in the project:
classes, functions, lists, dictionaries, sets, loops, file input/output, and
unit tests. Linear algebra is useful for the AR chapters, but the required
ideas are introduced when they first appear. Prior experience with machine
learning is not assumed.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{lecturebox}{Learning outcomes}
By the end of the course, a student should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item translate a geometric statement into the repository's formal
          construction-and-predicate language;
    \item distinguish a numerical diagram, which proposes and checks generic
          configurations, from a checkable symbolic proof;
    \item explain and implement the interaction between DD
          (deductive database) and AR (algebraic reasoning);
    \item generate structured random diagrams and turn consequences with
          replayable symbolic proofs into auditable training examples;
    \item train and evaluate a small language model whose only task is to
          propose auxiliary constructions; and
    \item report enough information for another group to interpret and repeat
          an experiment: which theorem rules were enabled, which examples were
          used for training, validation, and testing, how much time and search
          was allowed, and whether unsuccessful runs saturated, timed out, or
          failed because a construction was invalid.
\end{itemize}
\end{lecturebox}

The resulting program is an \emph{AlphaGeometry-style prototype}: it preserves
the architecture that matters pedagogically and can be scaled to more data and
compute, but it is not claimed to reproduce DeepMind's private training corpus
or its published performance.  The original AlphaGeometry paper is our primary
reference for this architecture \cite{trinh2024solving}; AlphaGeometry2 is a
later and broader system \cite{chervonyi2025gold}.

During the original offering, the coding project was deliberately open-ended.
That freedom produced creative designs, but it also allowed early interface
choices to create difficult bugs several weeks later.  These notes therefore
separate a small required core from optional extensions.  The interfaces,
invariants, and tests define the core; students remain free to change data
structures, add rules, improve search, or compare another language model once
the core behavior is preserved.

\begin{lecturebox}{How to read these notes}
On a first pass, follow the computational spine: the overview and architecture,
angle convention, formal objects and predicates, DD+AR, auxiliary
constructions, synthetic data, the guided reading of AlphaGeometryRE, and our
prototype.  The longer classical-geometry and linear-programming sections are
mathematical laboratories and optional deep dives; they can be read when their
ideas are used in lecture.  Code listings are not substitutes for running the
tests: students should predict an outcome, execute the example, and explain
any discrepancy.
\end{lecturebox}

\noindent\textbf{A statement on AI usage.}
In the initial course, students were encouraged to use AI as a programming and
writing aid.  AI tools were also used when drafting some diagrams, prose, code
outlines, and tests in these notes.  Every such artifact must be treated as an
untrusted proposal: a human remains responsible for checking the mathematics,
running the tests, citing sources, and being able to explain the submitted
work.

\section{AlphaGeometry Overview}
AlphaGeometry is a \keyterm{neuro-symbolic} geometry solver. Its learned
component searches for useful ideas; its symbolic components decide whether
those ideas lead to a valid proof. This division matters because a plausible
language-model response is not a valid proof, while a purely symbolic search
may never introduce the auxiliary point that makes an olympiad proof short.

The architecture can be remembered in three lines:
\[
\boxed{\text{DD = discrete theorem application}},\qquad
\boxed{\text{AR = algebraic closure}},\qquad
\boxed{\text{LM = construction search}}.
\]
DD matches configured theorem rules against known predicates and records each
rule application. AR combines justified angle and ratio equations by linear
algebra, avoiding long chains of small rearrangement rules. When these
symbolic engines get stuck, the language model tries to supply the crucial
idea by creating possible auxiliary constructions. We call these constructions
\keyterm{rabbits}, following the image of a magician pulling a useful object
from a hat. The model may create several candidates and rank them so that the
solver tries the most promising rabbits first.

The solver first runs DD and AR over the points already present. If they prove
the goal, proof traceback returns a valid symbolic proof. If they saturate
without proving it, the language model proposes one or more rabbits. Each
proposal is parsed, checked for existence and non-degeneracy, constructed
numerically, and translated into defining symbolic predicates. DD+AR then
runs again. A branch counts as solved only if this second symbolic run derives
the goal; the language model's confidence is never used as proof. It is also
possible that none of the attempted rabbits leads to a proof. The result is
then simply ``no proof found within this search,'' not a proof that the theorem
is false. Whenever the system does print a proof, its conclusion is correct
provided that the implemented symbolic rules, algebraic operations, and proof
checker are themselves correct.

Training data is created by running this process backward. We first build a
rich valid configuration, use DD+AR to derive consequences with recorded
symbolic justifications, and select a
consequence whose traced proof uses a chosen construction. We then hide that
construction. The visible state and goal become the model input, and the
hidden \texttt{Construction.py} call becomes the target. Later chapters explain
why one traced proof is only a candidate and why a stricter visible-versus-
restored solver test is valuable.

\begin{remark}
    This deliberately simplified example does not actually require a rabbit
    if the rule library can compare the triangle with the same triangle under
    a different vertex correspondence:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \verb|[1] ncoll A B C|
        \item \verb|[2] cong A B A C|
        \item \verb|[3] cong B C C B| (the same base segment with its endpoints reversed),
        \item \verb|[4] cong C A B A| (the symmetric form of \verb|[2]|),
        \item \verb|[5] contri* A B C A C B| (SSS with the reflected correspondence $A\leftrightarrow A$, $B\leftrightarrow C$, $C\leftrightarrow B$; the graph resolves it to \verb|contri2|).
        \end{itemize}
    This shows that there are subtleties when triangles involve the same set
    of points. Predicate symmetries must be handled deliberately: the order in
    \verb|contri A B C A B C| records a correspondence and cannot be sorted
    away.  The repository distinguishes \verb|contri|, \verb|contri2|, and
    the orientation-unspecified form \verb|contri*| (and similarly for
    \verb|simtri|).
\end{remark}

\section{Architecture of the Course Solver}
\label{sec: Architecture Course Solver}
This section describes the deliberately simple prover that students build
throughout the semester. It is not yet the richer object graph in the
AlphaGeometryRE-derived repository studied in Part III. Beginning with the
smaller design makes every component inspectable and lets us see exactly why a
more mature implementation might later make different tradeoffs.

\begin{lecturebox}{Goals for this section}
After reading this overview, you should be able to explain the points-only
design, distinguish numerical data from symbolic facts, and describe how the
semester project grows from a point class into a hybrid DD+AR+LM solver.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{lecturebox}{The recurring slogan}
\begin{center}
\textbf{Points $\longrightarrow$ relations between points
$\longrightarrow$ DD+AR.}
\end{center}
When symbolic reasoning gets stuck, the learned component proposes
\emph{new points}; DD+AR then proves new relations between all available
points. The language model suggests the crucial idea; the symbolic system
must check it and produce the proof.
\end{lecturebox}

\subsection*{The system students progressively build}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  node distance=4mm,
  stage/.style={draw, rounded corners, align=center, inner sep=4pt,
                text width=0.58\textwidth},
  >=stealth]
  \node[stage] (points) {points with numerical coordinates};
  \node[stage, below=of points] (preds) {predicates relating named points};
  \node[stage, below=of preds] (state) {problem state: givens, known facts,
                                        dependencies, and goal};
  \node[stage, below=of state] (dd) {DD: apply geometric rules};
  \node[stage, below=of dd] (ar) {AR: combine angle and ratio equations};
  \node[stage, below=of ar] (solver) {DDAR solver: alternate both engines until

  \node[stage, below=of lm] (hybrid) {hybrid solver: try rabbits, rebuild, and
                                      produce a symbolic proof when possible};

The crucial design choice is that \emph{points are the only geometric objects
whose lifecycle the course implementation manages}. A phrase such as ``line
$AB$'' is useful mathematical language, but it does not imply a stored
\texttt{Line} object. The predicate \verb|perp A B C D| relates four points
directly. Likewise, the angle variable $d(A,B)$ and log-length variable
$\ell(A,B)$ are indexed by point pairs rather than by separate line or segment
objects. This is a simplification made for the undergraduate implementation.
The actual AlphaGeometry-style reference implementation studied in Part~III
does store explicit objects for lines, segments, circles, angles, ratios, and
other geometric values. \Cref{sec: Basic Geometric Objects} develops the
course design, and Part~III explains the benefits and costs of the richer one.

Coordinates and symbolic facts deliberately coexist. Coordinates let us draw
a generic instance, reject an impossible construction, and prioritize
plausible conjectures. They do \emph{not} justify a theorem: a floating-point
coincidence in one picture is not a universal argument. A fact may enter a
proof only as a given, as the conclusion of a DD rule, or as a consequence
derived by AR with a recorded symbolic justification.

\subsection*{The solving loop}

For a formalized problem, the system follows this loop:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  node distance=8mm and 9mm,
  box/.style={draw, rounded corners, align=center, inner sep=5pt,
              text width=0.19\textwidth},
  decision/.style={draw, diamond, aspect=2.1, align=center, inner sep=2pt},

  \draw[->] (rebuild.north) -- (ddar.south);

The language model normally generates more than one possible construction.
Its scores provide an ordering, not a proof: the controller tries candidates
in that order, and only DD+AR can establish that one of them solves the
problem.
The loop is bounded: students choose a maximum number of proposed rabbits and
a time or iteration budget for each symbolic run. Therefore ``unsolved'' means
``not solved by this configured system within this budget,'' not
``mathematically impossible.'' Part III introduces the additional decoder and
beam-search limits used by the later reconstruction.

\subsection*{The data-and-training loop}

At classroom scale, training data reverses the direction of ordinary proof
search:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item perform one valid random construction that introduces a point $X$;
    \item run DDAR and find an interesting conclusion that does not mention
          $X$;
    \item check that the stored proof of that conclusion depends on facts
          introduced with $X$; and
    \item record the visible setup and goal as input, with the construction of
          $X$ as the target rabbit.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{lecturebox}{Concrete example: build first, then hide the rabbit}
Return to the isosceles triangle from the one-minute example. During data
generation we work in the opposite direction from ordinary solving. First we
start from $AB=AC$ and actually call
\verb|angle_bisector(C, A, B)|. The routine constructs $X$ on $BC$, draws the
bisector of $\angle BAC$, and inserts the corresponding collinearity and
equal-angle facts. DD+AR then proves the equality of the two base angles using
a derivation that passes through those new facts.

Once the proof has been found, the generator hides $X$ and its defining
facts. The learning pair is then
\begin{lstlisting}
"input_text":
  "Set_Up: ncoll A B C; cong A B A C;
   Goal: eqangle B A B C C B C A"
"output_text":
  "angle_bisector(C, A, B)"
\end{lstlisting}
The input contains only the visible problem; the output is the missing idea.
With a small rule library, this is a useful classroom candidate. A stronger
SSS rule may already prove the visible problem, which is precisely why the
later strict filter reruns DD+AR without $X$ before accepting the row as a
genuinely needed rabbit.
\end{lecturebox}

This simple test teaches the central idea, but one proof using $X$ does not
show that the solver truly needs $X$: another proof may avoid it. We postpone
strict filtering, global deduplication, leakage-safe splitting, large shards,
and production training formats until Part III, after the basic mapping is
understood.

\end{conceptcheck}

\section{A Small Python Survival Guide}\label{sec: Python Survival Guide}

The project does not require advanced Python. It repeatedly uses a small set
of ideas, shown together in the following points-only sketch:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle]
class Point:
    def __init__(self, name, x, y):
        self.name = name
        self.x = x
        self.y = y

def coll_key(a, b, c):
    return ("coll",) + tuple(sorted([a.name, b.name, c.name]))

points = {"A": Point("A", 0.0, 0.0)}       # dictionary: name -> point
known_facts = {("ncoll", "A", "B", "C")} # set: no duplicate keys
parents = {}                                  # fact -> justification

with open("problem.txt", encoding="utf-8") as source:
    problem_text = source.read()

def test_coll_key_ignores_order():
    a, b, c = Point("A", 0, 0), Point("B", 1, 0), Point("C", 2, 0)
    assert coll_key(a, b, c) == coll_key(c, a, b)
\end{lstlisting}

The recurring tools are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a \keyterm{class} groups the data and operations belonging to one
          kind of entity; initially, that entity is a point;
    \item a \keyterm{list} keeps an order, while a \keyterm{set} stores unique
          hashable items and supports fast membership tests;
    \item a \keyterm{dictionary} maps keys to values, such as a point name to
          its object or a fact key to its proof parents;
    \item a \keyterm{function} gives one operation a name and a testable
          input/output contract;
    \item loops apply an operation to many rules, facts, or candidate points;
    \item a \verb|with open(...)| block reads or writes a file and closes it
          safely; and
    \item a \keyterm{unit test} constructs a small example and uses
          \verb|assert| to state the behavior that must remain true.
\end{itemize}

When a Python feature is unfamiliar, first write a ten-line experiment that
prints or asserts its behavior. Small tests are especially important for
predicate symmetries: code that runs without an exception can still represent
the wrong mathematics.

\begin{conceptcheck}{python-survival}
Why is a dictionary useful for finding a point by name, while a set is useful
for storing canonical fact keys? What does the unit test above establish, and
what does it not establish about collinearity itself?
\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Angle Conventions}\label{sec: Angle Conventions}

The familiar interior angle of a triangle is convenient for people, but it is
awkward for algebraic proof search: replacing one point by another point on the
same line can change an interior angle to its supplement.  AlphaGeometry avoids
case distinctions by using \emph{directed line angles modulo $\pi$}.  We first
recall the ordinary convention and then explain the computational one.

The range of $\angle_0$ is $[0,\pi]$; the interior angles of a nondegenerate
triangle lie in $(0,\pi)$. We also have that
$\angle_0$ satisfies $\angle_0ABC = \angle_0CBA$. Now in terms of $\angle_0$,
the statement that the sum of the angles of a triangle add up to $\pi$ can be
interpreted as the following proposition.

\begin{prop}
    Given a triangle $ABC$, then $\angle_0ABC + \angle_0BCA + \angle_0CAB =
    \pi$.
\end{prop}
This statement is independent of whether $A,B,C$ run clockwise or
counter-clockwise because an ordinary angle is unchanged when the order of its
two rays is reversed.

When a transversal crosses parallel lines, people identify equal angles from
their positions in the picture. A formal rule using $\angle_0$ must instead
state the relevant collinearity, betweenness, and side-of-line information.
The following corresponding-angle rule is one representative example.

\begin{prop}[Corresponding Angles]\label{prop: Corresonding Angles for Angle 0}

    respect to the line $BB'C$, and $C$ does not lie between $B$ and $B'$, then
    $\angle_0ABC = \angle_0A'B'C$.

The familiar alternate-interior and alternate-exterior statements follow from
the same corresponding-angle relation after replacing one or both transversal
rays by their opposite rays.  With ordinary angles, that replacement may turn
an angle into its supplement.  Consequently a formal version must record which
points lie on which rays, which points lie between the intersections, and
whether the relevant points are on the same or opposite sides of the
transversal.  The names ``alternate interior'' and ``alternate exterior'' do
not by themselves contain enough information for a program to select the
correct ordinary angles.  This bookkeeping burden is the central reason for
moving to directed line angles modulo $\pi$ below.

\begin{defn}\label{defn: congruent triangles}
    Two triangles $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$ are congruent if $AB =
    DE$, $BC = EF$, $CA = FD$, $\angle_0ABC = \angle_0DEF$, $\angle_0BCA =
    \angle_0EFD$, and $\angle_0CAB = \angle_0FDE$.
\end{defn}
\noindent We have some classical results involving triangles. 
\begin{prop}[Euclid Book I Prop 4]\label{prop: Euclid Book 1 Prop 4 Angle 0} 
    If $AB = DE$, $BC = EF$, $\angle_0 ABC = \angle_0DEF$, then $\triangle ABC$
    and $\triangle DEF$ are congruent. This is the classical ``side-angle-side''
    triangle congruence rule.
\end{prop}

\begin{prop}[Euclid Book I Prop 5]\label{prop: Euclid Book 1 Prop 5 Angle 0}
    If $AB = AC$, then $\angle_0ABC = \angle_0ACB$. 
\end{prop}


\subsection{Angle 1: directed line angle modulo $\pi$}

Now we will describe a different way of measuring angles. This will be the
measurement that we will use when building our AI. In particular, it will be
important to see that there are differences in the statements of the
propositions between the different kinds of angle conventions.

\begin{defn}

\begin{remark}
    Modulo $\pi$ is forced by the fact that a line is unoriented: turning a
    direction through $\pi$ gives the same line.  The word ``directed'' still
    matters.  Interchanging the two lines negates the angle in
    $\mathbb{R}/\pi\mathbb{Z}$; it does not take an absolute value.
\end{remark}

The value is independent of which points name either supporting line, but it
depends on the order of the two lines.  Algebraically,
\[
  \angle_1ABC=-\angle_1CBA\pmod\pi.
\]
If both are displayed using representatives in $[0,\pi)$ and neither is zero,
those two representatives add to $\pi$.  Keeping the modular statement avoids
confusing a convenient display convention with the underlying definition.

The first advantage is that vertical angles no longer require a separate case.
If $A,B,A'$ are collinear and $C,B,C'$ are collinear, then the two pairs name
the same supporting lines, so
\[
    \angle_1 ABC=\angle_1 A'BC' \quad\text{in }\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z.
\]

\section{Reading the Course Formal Language}
\label{sec: Course Formal Language}

Before using a formal theorem as a running example, we need to know how to
read it. Part~I uses the deliberately simple language from the course
assignments. A problem has the form
\begin{center}
\codeseg{Set\_Up: fact 1; fact 2; ...; Goal: fact to prove}.
\end{center}
\verb|Set_Up| lists facts that are given at the start, semicolons separate
facts, and \verb|Goal| introduces the one fact the solver must derive. Capital
letters name points. A predicate name followed by point names is read as one
precise geometric statement.

The handouts and solver files also use a shorter separator:
\begin{center}
\codeseg{fact 1; fact 2; ... ? fact to prove}.
\end{center}
Everything before \verb|?| is the setup; the single predicate after
\verb|?| is the goal. Thus \verb|?| and the word \verb|Goal:| play the same
visual role in these notes. The question mark is a separator, not part of a
predicate and not a claim that the goal is already known. For example,
\begin{center}
\codeseg{ncoll A B C; cong A B A C ? eqangle B A B C C B C A}
\end{center}
is the compact spelling of the isosceles-triangle example below.

Here is the small vocabulary needed for the first examples:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.56\textwidth}}
\textbf{Course predicate} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\verb|coll A B C| & $A,B,C$ are collinear. \\
\verb|ncoll A B C| & $A,B,C$ are not collinear, so they form a
nondegenerate triangle. \\
\verb|cong A B C D| & the lengths are equal: $|AB|=|CD|$. The points and
segments do not become identical. \\
\verb|midp M A B| & $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$. \\
\verb|para A B C D| & line $AB$ is parallel to line $CD$. \\
\verb|perp A B C D| & line $AB$ is perpendicular to line $CD$. \\
\verb|cyclic A B C D| & $A,B,C,D$ lie on one circle. \\
\verb|eqangle A B C D E F G H| & the directed line angle from $AB$ to
$CD$ equals the directed line angle from $EF$ to $GH$, modulo $\pi$. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The eight arguments of \verb|eqangle| are easiest to read in four pairs:
\[
\verb|eqangle A B C D E F G H|
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\angle(AB,CD)=\angle(EF,GH)\pmod\pi.
\]
For example, the angle at $B$ in triangle $ABC$ is represented using lines
$BA$ and $BC$, while the angle at $C$ uses $CB$ and $CA$. Thus equality of
the base angles is written
\begin{center}
\codeseg{eqangle B A B C C B C A}.
\end{center}

The following examples translate complete natural-language theorems into the
course format.

\paragraph{Midpoint theorem.}
Natural language: in a nondegenerate triangle $ABC$, let $D$ and $E$ be the
midpoints of $AB$ and $AC$. Prove that $DE\parallel BC$.
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll A B C; midp D A B; midp E A C;
Goal: para D E B C
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Base angles of an isosceles triangle.}
Natural language: if $AB=AC$ in a nondegenerate triangle, prove that the
angles at $B$ and $C$ are equal.
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll A B C; cong A B A C;
Goal: eqangle B A B C C B C A
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{A point on a perpendicular bisector.}
Natural language: $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, and $XM\perp AB$. Prove that
$XA=XB$.
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll X A B; midp M A B; perp X M A B;
Goal: cong X A X B
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Thales's theorem in center--radius form.}
Natural language: $O$ is the midpoint of $AB$, and $OA=OC$. Prove that
$\angle ACB$ is a right angle.
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll A B C; midp O A B; cong O A O C;
Goal: perp A C B C
\end{lstlisting}

There is one further distinction. A line such as
\verb|midpoint(A, B)| is a \emph{construction call}: it creates a fresh point
$M$ and inserts the fact \verb|midp M A B|. The latter is a
\emph{predicate}: it is a statement already present in a setup or proof. This
distinction becomes essential when the language model learns to output a
construction call from a visible setup and goal.

\begin{lecturebox}{Course language versus reference language}
All examples in Part~I use the readable \verb|Set_Up|/\verb|Goal| format above
and \texttt{Construction.py} calls for rabbits. The AlphaGeometryRE-derived
repository has a different constructive syntax, such as clauses of the form
\verb|a b c = triangle a b c|. We postpone that language until Part~III and
will compare the two formats explicitly there.
\end{lecturebox}

\section{Basic Geometric Objects}\label{sec: Basic Geometric Objects}

\begin{lecturebox}{Design choice: points are the only geometric objects}
The course project deliberately manages only one kind of geometric entity:
\texttt{Point}. Unlike the richer AlphaGeometryRE architecture studied in
Part III, it does not create separate symbolic objects for lines, segments,
circles, angles, ratios, or triangles. Every formal relation is expressed
directly in terms of named points.
\end{lecturebox}

A point stores a name and numerical coordinates. As the project grows, it may
also store when and how it was constructed. Everything else belongs to one of
three categories:

\begin{center}

\hline
Primitive object & \verb|Point("A", x, y)| & The only geometric entity with
its own lifecycle and identity. \\
Derived numerical quantity & $d(A,B)$, $|AB|$, a slope, a circle residual, or
$|AB|/|CD|$ & Computed from point coordinates to draw, construct, reject
degeneracy, or suggest a conjecture. It is not a proof fact. \\
Symbolic fact & \verb|coll A B C|, \verb|cong A B C D|,
\verb|eqangle A B C D E F G H| & A mathematical relation among points,
stored with a justification and used by DD or AR. \\
Human shorthand & line $AB$, segment $AB$, circle through $A,B,C$, triangle
$ABC$ & Convenient mathematical language. It does not imply a corresponding
Python object in the course design. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For example, \verb|perp A B C D| means that the line determined by the pair
$(A,B)$ is perpendicular to the line determined by $(C,D)$. The implementation
stores the four point names in a predicate; it need not first construct two
\texttt{Line} objects. Similarly, $d(A,B)$ is a variable indexed by the pair
$(A,B)$ in the angle matrix, not a value node attached to a stored line.

This design is especially useful for beginning programmers. There is one
geometric entity to create, name, draw, and pass between modules, and every
predicate has the uniform interface ``a name plus point arguments.'' The cost
is redundancy: the pair $(A,B)$ may occur in many predicates and matrix
columns, and the solver must canonicalize point pairs repeatedly instead of
merging them through a shared line or segment object. Part III shows how
AlphaGeometryRE accepts greater implementation complexity to obtain richer
indices and object merging.

Coordinates are metadata, not axioms. Symbolically distinct points remain
distinct even if a poor realization places them close together. With $n$
points there are $\binom n2=O(n^2)$ point pairs and $O(n^4)$ pairs of point
pairs. The course solver therefore creates derived quantities only when a
predicate, rule, or AR equation needs them rather than materializing every
possible angle or ratio in advance.

We will revisit the following state as the solver gains new abilities:
\begin{center}
\codeseg{ncoll A B C; midp D A B; midp E A C}
\qquad $?$ \qquad
\codeseg{para D E B C}.
\end{center}
The only geometric objects are the five points $A,B,C,D,E$. The midpoint
relations are symbolic facts. Coordinates let us draw $D$ and $E$, but the
parallel conclusion must come from DD and AR.
\end{lecturebox}

\section{Basic Predicates}\label{sec: Basic Predicates}

A \keyterm{predicate} is a precisely defined relation applied to point names.
For example,
\begin{center}
\codeseg{coll A B C}\qquad
\codeseg{cong A B C D}\qquad
\codeseg{perp A B C D}
\end{center}
mean respectively that $A,B,C$ are collinear, that $AB=CD$, and that line
$AB$ is perpendicular to line $CD$. A formal statement has no hidden
information from the picture: if a condition matters to a rule, it must occur
as a predicate or as an explicitly checked non-degeneracy condition.

The core vocabulary can be organized by meaning.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.65\textwidth}}
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{Representative predicates} \\
\hline
Incidence & \verb|coll A B C|; \verb|cyclic A B C D|; and
\verb|circle O A B C|, which names a center $O$ for the circle through
$A,B,C$. \\
Direction & \verb|para A B C D| and \verb|perp A B C D| compare lines
$AB$ and $CD$. \\
Length and ratio & \verb|cong A B C D| asserts $AB=CD$;
\verb|eqratio A B C D E F G H| asserts
$AB/CD=EF/GH$. \\
Angle & \verb|eqangle A B C D E F G H| asserts that the directed line angle
from $AB$ to $CD$ equals that from $EF$ to $GH$, modulo $\pi$. \\
Named configurations & \verb|midp M A B| says that $M$ is the midpoint of
$AB$; \verb|simtri| and \verb|contri| record a specified correspondence
between two ordered triples. Orientation-reversing variants must be kept
distinct when the implementation uses them. \\
Non-degeneracy and orientation & \verb|ncoll A B C|, \verb|diff A B|,
\verb|npara A B C D|, and, when required, \verb|sameside| prevent a rule or
construction from silently using a degenerate configuration. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Many predicates have symmetries. For example, \verb|coll A B C| and
\verb|coll C A B| express the same fact. A database should not store them as
two different proof nodes. It therefore assigns each fact a
\keyterm{canonical key}: one agreed-upon database representation shared by
all argument orderings that have the same mathematical meaning.

For \verb|coll|, all six orderings of the three points can use the key
\verb|(coll, A, B, C)| after the point names have been placed in a fixed
order. For \verb|perp A B C D|, we may first order the endpoints within each
unoriented line and then order the two lines. Consequently,
\verb|perp D C B A| receives the same key as \verb|perp A B C D|. The key is
only a database identifier; it asserts no new geometry.

Canonicalization must follow the symmetry of the particular predicate. It is
not permission to sort every argument blindly. In
\verb|contri A B C P Q R|, the positions record the correspondences
$A\leftrightarrow P$, $B\leftrightarrow Q$, and $C\leftrightarrow R$.
Reordering is allowed only if those pairings and the relevant orientation are
preserved. Canonical keys prevent duplicate proof-graph nodes and make
membership tests and rule matching faster.

An efficient Python implementation can make these keys ordinary immutable
tuples. Tuples can be dictionary keys and set members, whereas lists cannot.
It is useful to write one small key function for each symmetry pattern rather
than placing special cases throughout DD:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def point_pair(a, b):
    return tuple(sorted((a, b)))

def coll_key(a, b, c):
    return ("coll",) + tuple(sorted((a, b, c)))

def perp_key(a, b, c, d):
    line_1 = point_pair(a, b)
    line_2 = point_pair(c, d)
    first, second = sorted((line_1, line_2))
    return ("perp",) + first + second
\end{lstlisting}
Store facts in a dictionary mapping each canonical key to its justification.
The expected cost of \verb|key in facts| is constant time. A dispatch
dictionary can route each predicate to its own canonicalizer, for example:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
KEY_FUNCTIONS = {"coll": coll_key,
                 "perp": perp_key}
\end{lstlisting}
Predicates with correspondence data, such
as \verb|contri|, need a separate function that enumerates only the rotations
or reflections that preserve the intended vertex matching and then chooses
the lexicographically smallest valid tuple. They must not use a generic
``sort all arguments'' function.

Unit tests should check both sides of the contract. Equivalent spellings such
as \verb|perp A B C D| and \verb|perp D C B A| must produce the same key;
facts with genuinely different pairings must produce different keys. Keeping
canonicalization in short pure functions makes these tests inexpensive and
prevents a subtle database bug from being confused with a geometry-rule bug.

Every accepted fact is stored with a justification. A given fact points back
to the problem statement. A derived fact records the rule or algebraic step and
the earlier facts it used. These parent links form the dependency graph used by
proof traceback. Numerical helper conditions such as ``two realized points are
too close'' may be stored for diagnostics, but they are not proof predicates.

For a concrete example, suppose the graph contains
\verb|perp A B C D| and \verb|perp C D E F| as justified facts. A DD rule may
add \verb|para A B E F|. The new parallelism node stores its canonical key,
the perpendicular-to-parallel rule that produced it, and links to the two
perpendicular parent nodes. Proof traceback follows those links back to the
givens. If another argument later derives \verb|para B A F E|,
canonicalization recognizes the same parallelism instead of creating a
duplicate node.

In the midpoint running example, constructing $D$ records facts equivalent to
\verb|coll A D B| and \verb|cong A D D B|; constructing $E$ records
\verb|coll A E C| and \verb|cong A E E C|. A midpoint-theorem rule, or a DD
rule unlocked by a ratio consequence from AR, may then derive
\verb|para D E B C|. The parallel fact stores links to the midpoint facts and
the \verb|ncoll A B C| guard, as well as to the named rule or algebraic
derivation, so traceback can explain the result.

\section{Problem Data Structure}\label{sec: Problem Data Structure}

\Cref{sec: Course Formal Language} introduced the external text that students
read and write. We now describe the data structure created after that text is
parsed. A compact input for the running midpoint example is
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll A B C; midp D A B; midp E A C;
Goal: para D E B C
\end{lstlisting}
This is the course format, not the constructive language of the reference
repository studied in Part~III. Some early assignment sheets use \verb|col|;
throughout these notes the parser normalizes that spelling to \verb|coll|.

Internally, the course state contains:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a dictionary from point names to \texttt{Point} records;
    \item a set or dictionary of facts indexed by canonical predicate keys;
    \item a justification for every given or derived fact;
    \item one goal predicate; and
    \item optional numerical drawing data, such as the coordinates read from
          a GeoGebra file.
\end{itemize}

The parser checks predicate names, arities, point references, and the single
goal marker.  Later, when a construction introduces a new point, the builder
must additionally require a fresh name and already-existing input points.
That rule keeps the construction history acyclic and reproducible.

Keep the following representations separate:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the original source text and source positions, used for helpful
          syntax errors;
    \item the parsed point dictionary, predicate facts, and goal;
    \item the numerical realization, used for drawing and construction
          validity checks; and
    \item the symbolic dependency graph and AR rows, which alone may certify
          the theorem.
\end{enumerate}
A failure to draw one realization should be reported as a construction or
numerical failure. It should not be interpreted as a symbolic disproof.

\section{Deductive Database}\label{sec: Deductive Database}

\begin{lecturebox}{Big idea}
DD is repeated theorem-pattern matching with proof bookkeeping.
\end{lecturebox}

A DD rule has premises and a conclusion. For example,
\begin{center}
\codeseg{perp A B C D}, \codeseg{perp C D E F}
$\Longrightarrow$ \codeseg{para A B E F}
\end{center}
expresses the familiar theorem that two lines perpendicular to the same line
are parallel, with appropriate non-degeneracy conditions included in the
actual rule. Capital letters in a rule are variables. DD searches the current
proof state for assignments of actual problem points that make every premise
known.

One breadth-first DD round performs three operations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Match:} find consistent assignments for a rule's variables;
    \item \textbf{Justify:} retrieve the symbolic dependencies that establish
          every premise; and
    \item \textbf{Add:} insert the canonical conclusion if it is new, together
          with the rule name and its parent facts.
\end{enumerate}
Rounds continue until the goal is present or no rule adds a new fact. The
latter condition is \keyterm{saturation}: a fixed point of the configured
rules over the current points. DD is sound only insofar as its rules and their
preconditions are correct, and it is not complete for all of Euclidean
geometry.

Naively trying every point assignment is expensive. Indices by predicate,
incident object, and equivalence class narrow the candidates. Canonical keys
prevent symmetric copies from being inserted repeatedly. A numerical diagram
may also prioritize plausible assignments, but DD must verify each premise in
the symbolic graph before applying a rule.

For the running example we choose to include the midpoint theorem as a DD
rule.  Its non-collinearity premise is essential:
\[
\verb|ncoll A B C|,\quad
\verb|midp D A B|,\quad \verb|midp E A C|
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\verb|para D E B C|.
\]
Here the capital letters are rule variables. Matching the three premises to
the five concrete points in the problem lets DD insert the goal together with
links to the non-collinearity guard and both midpoint facts. Course teams may choose a different theorem
library; the important implementation pattern is the same.

\begin{lecturebox}{Common pitfall}
A mathematically familiar rule may be false in a degenerate diagram. For
example, a triangle theorem requires non-collinearity. Preconditions such as
\verb|ncoll| are part of the theorem rule, not optional implementation details.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{conceptcheck}{dd}

established?
\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Generating Conjectures}\label{sec: Generating Conjectures}

Trying every possible relation among $n$ points quickly becomes infeasible.
The numerical realization can instead act as a search heuristic. We compute
quantities such as segment lengths, line directions, angle differences, and
circle residuals, and place approximately equal values into nearby buckets.
These buckets suggest candidate congruences, parallelisms, perpendicularities,
equal angles, or cyclic point sets.

This stage has a strict contract:
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{numerically plausible}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{worth asking the symbolic engine about},\\[2pt]
\text{but not}\qquad
\text{numerically plausible}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{proved}.
\end{array}
\]
Floating-point tolerances are not transitive, near-degenerate diagrams create
false coincidences, and one accidental realization can satisfy a relation that
does not follow from the assumptions. A candidate is promoted to a proof fact
only when DD, AR, or a construction definition supplies a symbolic
justification.

Useful safeguards are scale-aware tolerances, checking neighboring hash
buckets, refusing undefined or nearly degenerate objects, and retrying a
generated configuration with more than one random realization. Repeated
numerical agreement is still evidence for search, not formal proof.

There is a second use of candidate selection during data generation. After a
diagram has been closed under DD+AR, we may choose a nontrivial \emph{already
proved} fact as a synthetic theorem goal. In that setting the selection
heuristic chooses an interesting theorem from a certified closure; it does not
guess whether the theorem is true.

\begin{conceptcheck}{conjectures}

closure logically safer than choosing it directly from coordinate buckets?
\end{conceptcheck}


\section{Algebraic Reasoning}\label{sec: Algebraic Reasoning}

\begin{lecturebox}{Big idea}
AR replaces long chains of angle and ratio equalities by incremental linear
algebra.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{lecturebox}{Why add AR if DD already works?}
A solver can use DD alone, and DD by itself proves many problems. To reproduce
a long angle or ratio chase, however, a DD-only system needs rules for the
relevant transitivity, substitution, cancellation, and rearrangement steps.
It may create many intermediate facts while applying those rules one at a
time. AR packages this repeated equality chasing into linear algebra: it can
combine many already justified equations and expose in one calculation a
consequence that would require a long DD chain.

The advantage is a compact, reusable treatment of angle and ratio chasing.
The costs are an additional reasoning engine, proof-dependency bookkeeping for
row reduction, and a restricted scope: only relations that fit the chosen
linear representations belong in AR. DD remains better suited to discrete
geometric steps such as applying a circle theorem or recognizing similar
triangles. DD and AR are therefore complementary rather than competing
solvers.
\end{lecturebox}

The course implementation has exactly two AR tables: an angle table and a
ratio table.  Their variables are indexed directly by point pairs; no
\texttt{Line}, \texttt{Segment}, \texttt{Direction}, or \texttt{Length}
objects lie underneath them.

\subsection*{The angle matrix}

For distinct points $A,B$, let $d(AB)$ denote the direction of the line
determined by $A$ and $B$, modulo $\pi$. Since the line has no preferred
orientation, $d(AB)=d(BA)$ in $\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\verb|para A B C D|&:\quad d(AB)-d(CD)=0,\\
\verb|perp A B C D|&:\quad d(AB)-d(CD)=\frac{\pi}{2},\\
\verb|eqangle A B C D E F G H|&:\quad
d(AB)-d(CD)-d(EF)+d(GH)=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The coefficients form a row of the \keyterm{angle matrix}; an augmented
constant column records multiples of $\pi$.

Here \keyterm{provenance} means the recorded origin and derivation history of
a row: which accepted geometric facts were combined, and with what
coefficients, to obtain it. It is the algebraic version of parent links in the
DD proof graph. Provenance is not another geometric assumption. It is the
bookkeeping that lets the program turn a successful matrix calculation back
into a valid, inspectable proof.

Write the augmented constant as $q\pi$ with $q$ stored exactly as a rational
number. Because directions live modulo $\pi$, normalize $q$ modulo $1$ after
combining rows; one convenient canonical representative is
$q-\lfloor q\rfloor\in[0,1)$. Thus $0\pi$ and $1\pi$ encode the same
line-angle relation, while $\tfrac12\pi$ remains nonzero and represents a
right angle. In particular, adding two perpendicularity rows produces a
constant $1$, which normalizes to $0$ and exposes a parallelism.

There is a mathematical caution behind the implementation. The quotient
$\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z$ is not an ordinary real vector space: blindly
dividing a modular equation can change its solution set. A safe classroom
version either uses only logically valid row combinations such as addition
and subtraction, or keeps a consistent real lift selected by the numerical
realization and records that lift. The small elimination below uses only row
addition.

For example, suppose three line directions $L_0,L_1,L_2$ satisfy
\[
r_1:d(L_0)-d(L_1)=\frac{2}{9}\pi,
\qquad
r_2:d(L_1)-d(L_2)=\frac{1}{6}\pi.
\]
The matrix and its provenance columns may be displayed as
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrr|c|cc}
 &d(L_0)&d(L_1)&d(L_2)&q& r_1&r_2\\ \hline
r_1&1&-1&0&2/9&1&0\\
r_2&0&1&-1&1/6&0&1\\ \hline
r_3=r_1+r_2&1&0&-1&7/18&1&1
\end{array}
\]
The last row proves a $7\pi/18=70\degree$ line-angle relation. Its provenance
vector $(1,1)$ says that this row was obtained by using one copy of $r_1$ and
one copy of $r_2$. Those two accepted facts must therefore appear in the proof
traceback. In a larger elimination, the provenance coefficients are updated
by exactly the same row operations as the geometric coefficients.

\subsection*{The ratio matrix}

For distinct points $A,B$, introduce $\ell(AB)=\log |AB|$.  Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\verb|cong A B C D|&:\quad \ell(AB)-\ell(CD)=0,\\
\verb|eqratio A B C D E F G H|&:\quad
\ell(AB)-\ell(CD)-\ell(EF)+\ell(GH)=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Logarithms turn multiplication and division into addition and subtraction.
Zero-length pairs must be rejected before this representation is used.

Each table stores a basis of known equations.  When a new row arrives, the
solver reduces it against the existing rows.  A zero remainder was already
implied; otherwise the independent remainder is inserted.  Thus ``keep the
matrix full rank'' means that the stored rows remain linearly independent. It
does not mean that the matrix is square or has full column rank.

AR has the same proof obligation as DD. It must retain enough information to
explain each derived equation as a linear combination of previously justified
facts. The reduced table answers \emph{whether} an equality follows; a
separate dependency calculation answers \emph{why}. Only the dependencies
used in that calculation belong in the printed proof.

\begin{lecturebox}{Running example: what does AR learn?}
The two midpoint predicates imply
\[
\verb|cong A D D B|,\qquad \verb|cong A E E C|,
\]
so the ratio matrix receives
\[
\ell(AD)-\ell(DB)=0,\qquad \ell(AE)-\ell(EC)=0.
\]
In our minimal theorem library, DD applies the midpoint rule directly to prove
\verb|para D E B C|; the angle matrix can then record
$d(DE)-d(BC)=0$. This example is useful because not every problem needs every
component equally. Without the direct DD rule, an AR-assisted proof would need
an additional geometric rule connecting proportional division of two sides
to parallelism.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{lecturebox}{Where AR earns its keep: a $40\degree$--$30\degree$ chase}
Let $AB\parallel CD$, and let the broken transversal $A-E-C$ make a
$40\degree$ line-angle with $AB$ at the top and a $30\degree$ line-angle with
$CD$ at the bottom, with the signs fixed by the chosen directed-line
convention. Parallelism identifies the direction variables for $AB$ and
$CD$. The two given rows therefore have the form of $r_1$ and $r_2$ above,
and their sum gives the $70\degree$ angle between $AE$ and $CE$.

A DD-only solver can prove the same result, but it needs explicit rules to
transport angles across parallel lines, introduce an intermediate angle, add
the two constants, and remove the intermediate line. AR performs that equality
chase as one justified elimination. This is the kind of repeated algebra for
which AR complements DD; the midpoint theorem above remains an example that
the chosen DD library already handles directly.
\end{lecturebox}

The course AR stops with these two matrices.  The richer reference
implementation in Part~III also has a signed-distance table for collinear
points.  Keeping that distinction explicit prevents the larger code base from
silently changing the design that students were asked to build.

\section{The Basic Solver}\label{sec: Basic Solver}

The basic solver combines the components above. A simplified controller is:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle]
problem = parse(input_text)
diagram = realize_numerically(problem)
graph = build_symbolic_graph(problem, diagram)

while within_resource_budget():
    add_all_new_AR_consequences(graph)
    if goal_is_known(graph):
        return trace_proof(graph)

    new_facts = one_breadth_first_DD_round(graph)
    if goal_is_known(graph):
        return trace_proof(graph)
    if not new_facts and AR_has_no_new_consequences(graph):
        return SATURATED

return RESOURCE_LIMIT
\end{lstlisting}
The implementation may interleave DD and AR more finely, but the invariant is
the same: every fact in the graph has a symbolic justification, and every new
fact from either engine is made available to the other.

On the running midpoint example, the two midpoint facts are present initially.
The chosen midpoint DD rule adds \verb|para D E B C|, the goal test succeeds,
and traceback returns the two midpoint facts, the required
\verb|ncoll A B C| premise, and that rule application.

The solver should report at least four outcomes separately:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{proved}: the goal is in the graph and traceback succeeded;
    \item \textbf{saturated}: no configured inference produced a new fact;
    \item \textbf{resource limit}: a time or depth bound stopped the search;
    \item \textbf{invalid realization}: a construction could not be built
          reliably in the sampled numerical diagram.
\end{itemize}
Only the first is a proof. In particular, a timeout must never be relabeled as
evidence that an auxiliary construction is necessary.

Unit tests should check not only that a goal is found, but that every printed
step refers to earlier facts, names an allowed rule or algebraic derivation,
and respects all non-degeneracy conditions. Small negative tests are valuable:
removing one required premise should stop the corresponding rule from firing.

\begin{conceptcheck}{basic-solver}
Why do DD and AR have to share newly derived facts rather than run once in two
independent phases? Which of the four solver outcomes may be used as a positive
training label?
\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Auxiliary Constructions}\label{sec: Auxiliary Constructions}

An auxiliary construction introduces one or more new points from points that
already exist. Examples include a midpoint, a perpendicular foot, an
intersection of two lines, a point on a circle, an incenter, or an
orthocenter. It is best to regard each routine as a checked state transition,
not merely as a coordinate formula:
\[
\boxed{\text{check preconditions}}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{construct }X}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{register }X}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{insert defining predicates}}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{update AR}}.
\]
The first step checks that the named input points exist and that the requested
object is well defined. The second computes a stable numerical realization.
The third assigns every output a fresh name and adds its \texttt{Point} record.
The fourth inserts symbolic facts with construction justifications. Finally,
because the course AR tables are indexed by point pairs, the fifth step creates
the necessary columns involving the new point and registers every angle or

Construction branches require special care. Two circles can meet in zero,
one, or two points; two lines may be parallel; and nearly parallel lines may
produce an unstable numerical intersection. If a construction has multiple
valid outputs, the formal language must specify a deterministic convention or
introduce all outputs with the relations that distinguish them. A model output
must also use fresh point names and may refer only to visible points or points
introduced earlier in the same proposal.

When the basic solver saturates, an auxiliary proposer returns a small ranked
list of \texttt{Construction.py} calls. For each proposal the controller runs
all five transition steps above and then reruns DD+AR.
A proposal is useful only if this final symbolic run proves the goal. The
language model is therefore a search policy, not a proof oracle.

\section{Generating Synthetic Data}\label{sec: Generating Synthetic Data}

We now have the ingredients needed to manufacture examples of useful
auxiliary constructions. The key is to randomize \emph{valid constructions},
not unrelated coordinates. Random coordinates almost never create exact
collinearities, perpendicularities, equal lengths, or concyclicities. A
midpoint, foot, or intersection deliberately creates several connected facts.

\begin{lecturebox}{The classroom goal}
We want examples of the form
\[
(\text{visible problem state},\text{goal})
\longmapsto
\text{a useful new construction }X.
\]
The left side is the visible state presented to the future solver. After the
strict filtering introduced in Part~III, it will be a state on which the
configured DD+AR solver has genuinely saturated without proving the goal. The
right side is the rabbit that a language model should learn to propose.
\end{lecturebox}

A first classroom generator has six steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item begin with a small valid configuration, such as a triangle;
    \item apply several randomly selected constructions whose inputs already
          exist;
    \item add one more valid construction introducing a point $X$;
    \item run DD+AR on the completed diagram;
    \item attempt to choose a nontrivial proved conclusion that does not
          mention $X$ and whose traced proof uses a defining fact of $X$; and
    \item hide the construction of $X$ and record it as the target output.
\end{enumerate}

Step~5 can fail, and this is an important part of the generator rather than a
minor implementation error. A valid new point may lead only to facts that name
$X$, DD+AR may derive nothing interesting from its defining facts, or every
interesting conclusion may already have a proof that avoids $X$. The program
must then record a ``no candidate conclusion'' outcome and try another
construction or another diagram; it must not invent a goal or weaken the
dependency test. Useful rabbit data is therefore much rarer than valid random
geometry. Structured construction families, connected diagrams, several
candidate goals per completed closure, and careful failure statistics improve
the yield, but they do not guarantee that every generated diagram supplies a
training row.

For a first implementation, choose $X$ as the \emph{last} constructed point.
Then hiding it cannot leave a later construction referring to an undefined
input. The general dependency-closure operation is postponed to Part~III.

\subsection*{A running rabbit example}

Homework~9 uses the course problem serialization on the input side and an
actual call to the team's \texttt{Construction.py} library on the output side.
For example, a training record can be written as
\begin{lstlisting}
{
  "input_text":
    "Set_Up: cong A B A C; Goal: eqangle C B A B C A",
  "output_text": "angle_bisector(C, A, B)"
}
\end{lstlisting}
This is the representation printed in the Homework~9 handout. The setup says
that $AB=AC$, the goal asks for equality of the two base angles, and the target
is the exact \texttt{Construction.py} call that creates the angle-bisector
rabbit. A team's actual function may also receive the current problem object;
if so, that argument belongs in the target string as well. The handout used a
six-point spelling for equality of two vertex angles. A team using the
eight-point directed-line predicate defined earlier must serialize the
equivalent predicate in its own parser's syntax and use that choice
consistently throughout the corpus.

This makes the backward nature of data generation concrete. The generator
first calls construction functions to build a larger diagram, runs DD+AR, and
selects a certified conclusion whose proof uses the newest construction even
though the conclusion does not name its new point. It then hides that last
call. The reference repository's different construction language---for
example \verb|on_line| clauses---is introduced only in Part~III.

\begin{lecturebox}{A subtlety postponed to Part III}
One traced proof using $X$ does not show that DD+AR truly needs $X$: another
proof might avoid it. The classroom generator therefore creates
\emph{candidate rabbits}. Our repository prototype later adds a two-sided
strict test, dependency-aware hiding, canonicalization, global deduplication,
distribution audits, and parallel shards; see \Cref{sec: Our Prototype}.
\end{lecturebox}

\end{conceptcheck}

\section{A First Audit of the Data}\label{sec: Cleaning the data}

Even a classroom-sized generator should not accept every row it emits. At
minimum, perform these five checks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reconstruction.} Reparse both the visible and completed
          text. Every point must be defined before it is used, and the visible
          goal must remain well formed after $X$ is hidden.
    \item \textbf{Proof dependence.} Confirm that DD+AR proves the completed
          problem and that traceback uses a fact introduced with $X$.
    \item \textbf{Duplicates and provenance.} Avoid repeated rows and retain
          an identifier for the random source diagram.
    \item \textbf{Experimental separation.} Keep every example from one
          source diagram in the same training, validation, or test partition;
          otherwise near-duplicates leak into evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Failure records.} Count invalid constructions, failed
          realizations, resource limits, and failed tracebacks separately.
\end{enumerate}

These checks make the classroom experiment interpretable, but they do not yet
show that the rabbit is necessary for the configured solver. Part~III explains
strict visible-versus-restored solving, point-renaming canonicalization,
dependency closures, leakage-safe corpus preparation, and distributed shard
merging.

\begin{conceptcheck}{data-cleaning}
Why does choosing the hidden point last simplify the generator? Why should
rows from one source diagram remain in one partition? Why is successful
traceback not yet a strict test that the rabbit is needed?
\end{conceptcheck}
\section{Learning to Propose Rabbits}
\label{sec: Learning to Propose Rabbits}
\label{sec: Fine Tuning the Language Model}

\begin{lecturebox}{The model has one job}
Given a visible formal problem and its goal, the language model ranks one or
more constructions that introduce new points:
\[
\boxed{\text{visible state and goal}}
\xrightarrow{\text{language model}}
\boxed{\text{candidate rabbits}}.
\]
It is not asked to certify the theorem. DD+AR remains responsible for proof.
\end{lecturebox}

Here ``problem state'' means the chosen formal serialization, normally the
visible construction history together with the goal. It need not contain every
fact in the full DD+AR closure, but the same format must be used in training
and inference. We compare two ways of teaching this behavior: few-shot
prompting and fine-tuning.

\subsection{Fine-tuning}

In \keyterm{fine-tuning}, many input-output pairs are shown during training
and the model parameters are adjusted to make the desired construction more
likely. The Homework~9 example becomes
\begin{lstlisting}
"input_text":
  "Set_Up: cong A B A C; Goal: eqangle C B A B C A"
"output_text":
  "angle_bisector(C, A, B)"
\end{lstlisting}
One row only explains the format. Learning the task requires varied diagrams,
goals, and construction types. Split the data into training examples, which
update parameters; validation examples, which choose settings and checkpoints;
and a test set reserved for final measurement.

Fine-tuning needs substantial data and compute but can specialize a model to
the formal language and keep per-query prompts short. The original course used
a Gemma tutorial as an accessible starting point and Brown students could run
training on OSCAR. Our later prototype instead specifies its own tokenizer,
architecture, checkpoints, and two training stages in
\Cref{sec: Our Prototype}; the central input-output task is unchanged.

\subsection{Few-shot prompting}\label{sec: n-shot prompting}

In \keyterm{few-shot prompting}, also called $n$-shot prompting, the model
parameters remain unchanged. Instructions and $n$ known examples are placed in
the prompt before the new query:
\begin{lstlisting}
TASK:
Given a formal geometry state and goal, propose one valid
construction introducing a fresh point.

DEMONSTRATION INPUT:
Set_Up: cong A B A C; Goal: eqangle C B A B C A

DEMONSTRATION OUTPUT:
angle_bisector(C, A, B)

NEW INPUT:
<the current visible problem and goal>

OUTPUT:
\end{lstlisting}
The demonstrations teach the syntax and desired type of action without a
training run, so it is easy to exchange the underlying model. The tradeoff is
a longer, prompt-sensitive input with space for only finitely many examples.
No row used for final evaluation may also appear among the demonstrations.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}}
 & \textbf{Few-shot prompting} & \textbf{Fine-tuning} \\
\hline
Model parameters & unchanged & updated by training \\
Data requirement & a small demonstration set & a larger diverse corpus \\
Per-query prompt & longer & usually shorter \\
Changing models & relatively easy & usually requires new training \\
Main risk & prompt sensitivity & overfitting and data leakage \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{The symbolic validation loop}
\label{sec: Integrating the language model}

A model output is only a proposal. Process each candidate in a fixed order:
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{LM proposal}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{parse and validate}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{construct the point numerically}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{add the point and defining predicates}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{register new angle and ratio variables/equations}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{rerun DD+AR}\\ \downarrow\\
\text{accept only if the symbolic goal is proved}.
\end{array}
\]
Parsing checks the grammar, fresh names, and references to existing points.
Numerical realization checks existence and degeneracy. Because the course AR
indexes variables by point pairs, a newly introduced point also creates the
direction or log-length variables needed by its defining relations. A valid
but unhelpful proposal may remain as a search branch if the budget permits;
malformed or unrealizable proposals are rejected.

\subsection{A controlled comparison}

Compare construction policies under one theorem set and one search budget:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DD+AR only}: add no points;
    \item \textbf{random rabbit}: sample a legal construction;
    \item \textbf{expert heuristic}: try a fixed list of standard centers and
          intersections;
    \item \textbf{few-shot LM}: rank constructions from prompt examples; and
    \item \textbf{fine-tuned LM}: rank them with updated model parameters.
\end{itemize}
Report not only solved problems, but problems solved beyond DD+AR, valid
proposal rate, runtime, maximum added points, and every timeout or decoding
limit. Otherwise a larger search budget can masquerade as a better model.

\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Automatic Diagram Generation and Autoformalization}
\label{sec: Autoformalization}

This is an optional extension. The core course pipeline begins with a human
formalization and numerical diagram. An automated front end would instead try
to translate a natural-language statement into points, predicates,
constructions, and a goal, then produce one or more numerical realizations.

The convenience is obvious: a user can begin from an ordinary problem
statement. The danger is equally important. Omitting a hypothesis can make the
formal theorem false, while inventing a hypothesis can make it trivial. A
plausible picture does not show that the translation is faithful.

An acceptable front end should therefore expose checkable stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item parse the generated formal syntax;
    \item verify that every named point and predicate has the required arity;
    \item realize several generic, nondegenerate diagrams when possible;
    \item show the formal statement and diagrams to the user for comparison;
    \item send only the accepted formal problem to the symbolic solver.
\end{enumerate}
Independent language-model reviewers may catch some errors, but agreement
between agents is not a correctness criterion. Acceptance comes from explicit
parsing, realization checks, symbolic validation, and---for a faithful
translation---human or separately certified comparison with the source.

For automatic diagram generation, you may consider
\cite{krueger2021automatically}, whose
\href{https://github.com/rkruegs123/geo-model-builder}{open-source implementation}
can be integrated into a larger system. Other generators may be used if their
construction semantics and failure modes are documented.

\begin{conceptcheck}{autoformalization}
Give one example of an omitted assumption and one example of an invented
assumption that could corrupt a geometry formalization. Why do several matching
numerical diagrams provide useful evidence but not a proof of faithful
translation?
\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Power of a Point}
For the remainder of the notes (until we present the coding homeworks at the end
of the document), we shall present some of the tools/theorems in Euclidean
geometry. These concepts might not be used in your system, but you could try to
either build your system in such a robust way that you can prove these theorems,
or you could try to incorporate some of these theorems in your DDAR (although
that might be a bit tricky).

We begin this section by discussing the \textbf{power of a point} which is a
quantity that relates a point $P$ and a given circle.

\begin{defn}
    The \textbf{power of a point} $P$ with respect to a circle with center $O$
    and radius $OA$ is the quantity:
    \[
    \Pi(P)=PO^2 - OA^2.
    \]
\end{defn}

Although the formula names a point $A$ on the circle, $OA^2$ is simply the
squared radius and is independent of that choice. If $P$ lies outside the
circle and $PA$ is tangent at $A$, then $OA\perp PA$, so the Pythagorean
theorem gives $\Pi(P)=PA^2$.  An interior point has no real tangent, and its
power is negative.

Power of a point is quadratic, so it does not fit directly into the core
linear angle-and-ratio tables.  The following results are nevertheless useful:
they turn that quadratic quantity into products of lengths along secants and
chords.

\begin{prop}[Tangent--secant theorem]
Let \(P\) lie outside a circle with center \(O\).  Let \(PA\) be tangent at
\(A\), and let a secant from \(P\) meet the circle first at \(B\) and then at
\(C\).  Then
\[
    PA^2=PB\cdot PC=\Pi(P).
\]
In the formal vocabulary, the hypotheses include
\codeseg{perp P A A O}, equal radii to \(A,B,C\), and
\codeseg{coll P B C}.
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
By the tangent--chord theorem, the angle between tangent \(PA\) and chord
\(AB\) equals the inscribed angle subtending the same chord, so
\(\angle PAB=\angle PCA\).  Since \(P,B,C\) are collinear, triangles
\(\triangle PAB\) and \(\triangle PCA\) also have the same angle at \(P\).
They are therefore similar.  Reading corresponding sides gives
\[
    \frac{PA}{PC}=\frac{PB}{PA},
    \qquad\text{hence}\qquad
    PA^2=PB\cdot PC.
\]
Finally \(OA\perp PA\), so the Pythagorean theorem gives
\(PA^2=PO^2-OA^2=\Pi(P)\).
\end{proof}

We now turn to the case where $P$ is inside the circle, giving rise to the \textbf{intersecting chords theorem}. 
In particular, we remark that the assumption of \verb|perp P A A O| is superfluous, and it suffices to just prove the 
following. 

\begin{prop}[Intersecting Chords]
Let $P$ be a point inside a circle with center $O$, and let two chords through $P$ meet the circle at $A$, $B$ and $C$, $D$ respectively. Then
\[
    PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD = |\Pi(P)|.
\]
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
Choose $AB$ to be the chord passing through both $P$ and the center $O$, with $A$ the farther endpoint from $P$, so the order along the line is $B$-$P$-$O$-$A$. Let $CD$ be any other chord through $P$.

    % Segment labels
    %\node[above=2pt] at ($(P)!0.5!(A)$) {$PA$};
    %\node[above=2pt] at ($(P)!0.5!(B)$) {$PB$};
    %\node[above left=1pt] at ($(P)!0.5!(C)$) {$PC$};
    %\node[below left=1pt] at ($(P)!0.5!(D)$) {$PD$};

    % Points and labels
    \fill[black] (O) circle (2pt) node[below right, inner sep=2pt] {$O$};
    \fill[black] (P) circle (2pt) node[above right, inner sep=3pt] {$P$};
    \fill[black] (A) circle (2pt) node[right] {$A$};
    \fill[black] (B) circle (2pt) node[left] {$B$};
    \fill[black] (C) circle (2pt) node[above right] {$C$};
    \fill[black] (D) circle (2pt) node[below left] {$D$};

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Since $OA = OB = r$, we have $PO + OA = PA$ and $PO - OA = PO - OB = -PB$. Therefore,
\[
    \Pi(P) = PO^2 - OA^2 = (PO - OA)(PO + OA) = (-PB)(PA) = -PA \cdot PB.
\]
It suffices to show $PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$, for then $\Pi(P) = -PC \cdot PD = -|\Pi(P)|$, giving $|\Pi(P)| = PC \cdot PD$.

Since $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ all lie on the circle, the inscribed angles $\angle PCB$ and $\angle PAD$ both subtend arc $BD$ (\textcolor{red}{red}), so $\angle PCB = \angle PAD$; likewise $\angle PBC$ and $\angle PDA$ both subtend arc $CA$ (\textcolor{blue}{blue}). The angles $\angle CPB$ and $\angle APD$ are vertical angles at $P$ (\textcolor{green!60!black}{green}). By AA similarity,
\[
    \triangle PCB \sim \triangle PAD.
\]
Reading off corresponding sides:
\[
    \frac{PC}{PA} = \frac{PB}{PD} \implies PC \cdot PD = PA \cdot PB = |\Pi(P)|. \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}



\begin{remark}
    We now remark on the notion of signed length.
    In particular, we see that in the case where $P$ lies
    outside of the circle, we have that $\Pi(P)$ is positive, 
    and $\Pi(P) = PB * PC$. However, in the case where $P$ is 
    inside the circle, we have that $\Pi(P)$ is negative, and
    so $\Pi(P) = PB * PC$ doesn't entirely make sense if we are
    measuring $PB$ and $PC$ to both be positive lengths. However,
    to make sense of this since $P$ lies between the points $B$
    and $C$ on the line, if we choose an orientation of the line, 
    then one of these segments is positively orientated and the other
    is negatively orientated which explains why $\Pi(P)$ is negative
    for points inside the circle. 
\end{remark}

\section{Radical Axis}

We shall now discuss the radical axis, this relates the power of points between
two circles.

\begin{defn}
    For two distinct, nonconcentric circles, the \textbf{radical axis} is the
    locus of points $P$ satisfying $\Pi_1(P)=\Pi_2(P)$.  Subtracting the two
    power expressions shows that this locus is a line.  For coincident circles
    the locus is the whole plane; for distinct concentric circles it is empty,
    so neither exceptional case has a radical-axis line.
\end{defn}

We begin with some basic lemmas about the radical axis of two circles.

\begin{prop}
    Assume that circles $O_1$ and $O_2$ intersect at two points $A$ and $B$.
    Then the line $AB$ is the radical axis. 
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
We show that every point $P$ on line $AB$ satisfies $\Pi_1(P) = \Pi_2(P)$, treating separately the cases where $P$ lies outside versus inside the two circles.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8, font=\small\sffamily, line join=round]

    % O1=(-1,0) radius 2.5, O2=(1.5,0) radius 2
    % Radical axis at x=0.7; intersection points A=(0.7,1.83), B=(0.7,-1.83)
    % P1=(0.7,3.0) outside both circles, P2=(0.7,0) inside both circles
    \coordinate (O1) at (-1, 0);
    \coordinate (O2) at (1.5, 0);
    \coordinate (A)  at (0.7, 1.83);
    \coordinate (B)  at (0.7, -1.83);
    \coordinate (P1) at (0.7, 3.1);
    \coordinate (P2) at (0.7, 0);

    % Circles
    \draw[thick] (O1) circle (2.5);
    \draw[thick] (O2) circle (2);

    % Line AB extended through P1 above and slightly beyond B below
    \draw[thick] (P1) -- (0.7, -2.2);

    % Centers
    \fill[black] (O1) circle (2pt) node[below] {$O_1$};
    \fill[black] (O2) circle (2pt) node[below right] {$O_2$};

    % Points on the line
    \fill[black] (P1) circle (2pt) node[right] {$P_1$};
    \fill[black] (A)  circle (2pt) node[above right] {$A$};
    \fill[black] (P2) circle (2pt) node[right] {$P_2$};
    \fill[black] (B)  circle (2pt) node[below right] {$B$};

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{Case 1: $P_1$ on line $AB$, outside both circles.}
The line through $P_1$, $A$, $B$ is a secant to circle $O_1$ meeting it at $A$ and $B$. By the power of a point (tangent-secant theorem applied to the secant $P_1 A B$),
\[
    \Pi_1(P_1) = P_1A \cdot P_1B.
\]
The same line is also a secant to circle $O_2$ meeting it at the same points $A$ and $B$, so
\[
    \Pi_2(P_1) = P_1A \cdot P_1B.
\]
Hence $\Pi_1(P_1) = P_1A \cdot P_1B = \Pi_2(P_1)$.

\textbf{Case 2: $P_2$ on line $AB$, inside both circles.}
The segment $AB$ is a chord of circle $O_1$ passing through $P_2$. By the intersecting chords theorem,
\[
    \Pi_1(P_2) = -P_2A \cdot P_2B.
\]
The same chord lies inside circle $O_2$ as well, so
\[
    \Pi_2(P_2) = -P_2A \cdot P_2B.
\]
Hence $\Pi_1(P_2) = -P_2A \cdot P_2B = \Pi_2(P_2)$.

In both cases every point on line $AB$ has equal power with respect to the two circles, so $AB$ is the radical axis.
\end{proof}

We now state a theorem which describes the radical axes of three circles.

\begin{thm}[Radical-center theorem]
    Let $l_1,l_2,$ and $l_3$ be the radical axes of $O_2$ and $O_3$, $O_1$ and $O_3$, and $O_1$ and $O_2$ respectively.
    If two of these axes intersect, then all three are concurrent at that
    point.  Otherwise the three axes are parallel.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
    Let $P$ be on the intersection of $l_1$ and $l_2$. We claim that $P$ is on $l_3$. Since $P\in l_1$, we have that $\Pi_2(P)=\Pi_3(P)$. 
    Now since $P\in l_2$, we have that $\Pi_1(P) = \Pi_3(P)$. Thus, we will have that $\Pi_1(P)=\Pi_2(P)=\Pi_3(P)$. Thus, $P\in l_3$.

    In the case where each pair of circles intersects at two points, the three radical axes are precisely the lines through each pair of intersection points, and their concurrency at the radical center $R$ is illustrated below.

\end{proof}

The parallel case can equivalently be described projectively by saying that
the three axes meet at the same point at infinity.

Furthermore, using this theorem one can construct the radical axis of two non-intersecting circles. We leave this as an exercise. 


\section{Menelaus's Theorem}\label{sec: Menelaus}
We use directed lengths on each sideline.  After choosing an orientation of a
line, $\overline{XY}$ is positive when the direction from $X$ to $Y$ agrees
with that orientation and negative otherwise.  This convention makes one
statement cover points on sides and on their extensions.

\begin{thm}[Menelaus's theorem]
Let $D\in BC$, $E\in CA$, and $F\in AB$, with the usual nondegeneracy
conditions. Then $D,E,F$ are collinear if and only if
\[
 \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}
 \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}
 \frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=-1.
\]
In parser vocabulary the incidence hypotheses use \verb|coll|.  The displayed
formula uses directed, not ordinary unsigned, lengths.
\end{thm}

\begin{lecturebox}{Solver lens: Menelaus}
Collinearity is a symbolic incidence predicate; it is not fundamentally an
angle-table calculation. The course ratio table stores
$\ell(AB)=\log|AB|$, so it remembers multiplicative magnitudes but discards
the signs of directed ratios. It therefore cannot distinguish the Menelaus
product $-1$ from a product $+1$ using log magnitudes alone. A sound
implementation needs additional order/sign information or a dedicated DD
rule whose directed hypotheses are checked. This limitation is especially
instructive because the magnitude equation looks perfectly linear after
taking logarithms while still omitting essential geometry.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{proof}
The diagram and similarity calculation below prove one common point order using
positive segment lengths.  Translating each quotient to the directed convention
above contributes the required sign and gives the invariant product $-1$.

\textbf{Construction.} Let $X$ be the point on line $AB$ such that $XC \parallel DEF$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9, font=\small\sffamily, line join=round]

    % Coordinates from Geogebra (D chosen to right of C; E on AC at t=0.6;
    % F = Intersect(AB, Line(D,E)); X = Intersect(AB, line through C parallel to DE))
    \coordinate (A) at (-3.47, 4.47);
    \coordinate (B) at (-4.63, 0);
    \coordinate (C) at (-1.13, 0);
    \coordinate (D) at (0.5, 0);
    \coordinate (E) at (-2.07, 1.79);
    \coordinate (F) at (-3.84, 3.02);
    \coordinate (X) at (-4.09, 2.06);

    % Shaded fills drawn first (behind everything else)
    \fill[blue!15] (A) -- (X) -- (C) -- cycle;
    \fill[red!15]  (B) -- (F) -- (D) -- cycle;

    % Main triangle and extended base (thin black, on top of fills)
    \draw[thick] (A) -- (B);
    \draw[thick] (B) -- (D);   % base B-C-D (D beyond C)
    \draw[thick] (C) -- (A);

    % Menelaus transversal line through D, E, F (dashed)
    \draw[dashed, gray!80!black] (-4.33, 3.36) -- (0.91, -0.29);

    % Triangle AXC: bold blue outline
    \draw[line width=2pt, blue!70!black] (A) -- (X) -- (C) -- cycle;

    % Triangle BFD: bold red outline
    \draw[line width=2pt, red!70!black] (B) -- (F) -- (D) -- cycle;

    % Parallel marks: same-direction arrows at midpoints of EF and XC
    \draw[->, thick] ($(F)!0.45!(E)$) -- ($(F)!0.55!(E)$);
    \draw[->, thick] ($(X)!0.45!(C)$) -- ($(X)!0.55!(C)$);

    % Points and labels
    \fill[black] (A) circle (2pt) node[above right] {$A$};
    \fill[black] (B) circle (2pt) node[below left]  {$B$};
    \fill[black] (C) circle (2pt) node[below]        {$C$};
    \fill[black] (D) circle (2pt) node[below right]  {$D$};
    \fill[black] (E) circle (2pt) node[right]         {$E$};
    \fill[black] (F) circle (2pt) node[left]          {$F$};
    \fill[black] (X) circle (2pt) node[left]          {$X$};

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{Step 1: $\triangle AXC \sim \triangle AFE$ (blue triangle).}
Since $XC \parallel FE$, we have $\angle AXC = \angle AFE$ (corresponding angles) and $\angle A$ is shared. By AA similarity, $\triangle AXC \sim \triangle AFE$, giving $AX/AF = AC/AE$. Since the order along line $AB$ is $A$--$F$--$X$--$B$, we have $AX = AF + FX$ and $AC = AE + EC$, so
\[
    \frac{AF + FX}{AF} = \frac{AE + EC}{AE} \implies \frac{FX}{AF} = \frac{EC}{AE}.
\]

\textbf{Step 2: $\triangle BXC \sim \triangle BFD$ (red triangle).}
Since $XC \parallel FD$, we have $\angle BXC = \angle BFD$ (corresponding angles) and $\angle B$ is shared. By AA similarity, $\triangle BXC \sim \triangle BFD$, giving $BX/BF = BC/BD$. Since $BX = BF - FX$ and $BC = BD - CD$,
\[
    \frac{BF - FX}{BF} = \frac{BD - CD}{BD} \implies \frac{FX}{BF} = \frac{CD}{BD} \implies \frac{BD}{CD} = \frac{BF}{FX}.
\]

\textbf{Conclusion.} Substituting into the product:
\[
    \frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{CD}\cdot\frac{CE}{AE}
    = \frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BF}{FX}\cdot\frac{FX}{AF} = 1. 
\]

Now for the reverse direction, assume the directed product in the theorem is
$-1$.
Now we shall take the points $D$ and $E$, and extend this line to intersect $AB$ at a point $F'$. Our goal is
to prove that $F = F'$. In particular, since \verb|coll D E F'|, we have that
The forward direction gives the same directed product for $F'$.
Thus, we may conclude that 
\[
\frac{AF'}{F'B} = \frac{AF}{FB}.
\]
On a directed affine coordinate on $AB$, the map
$t\mapsto\overline{At}/\overline{tB}$ is injective away from $B$.
Consequently $F'=F$, completing the converse.
\end{proof}

\section{Ceva's Theorem}\label{sec: Ceva}

We now will give a proof of Ceva's theorem. 

\begin{thm}[Ceva's Theorem]
Let $D\in BC$, $E\in CA$, and $F\in AB$.  In the affine case, the lines
$AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ are concurrent if and only if
\[
 \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}
 \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}
 \frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=1.
\]
Here the bars denote directed lengths with cyclic orientations chosen on the
three sidelines.  In a projective formulation, three parallel cevians meet at
a point at infinity.
\end{thm}

\begin{lecturebox}{Solver lens: Ceva}
Menelaus and Ceva have nearly identical ratio magnitudes but different signs
and different incidence conclusions. Ceva's product is $+1$ and its conclusion
is concurrency, which a points-only solver can represent by constructing a
point $X$ and storing \verb|coll A D X|, \verb|coll B E X|, and
\verb|coll C F X|. The core log-ratio table alone cannot distinguish this case
from the unsigned magnitude of Menelaus. Signed sideline order, a checked
Ceva rule, or a richer algebraic representation must supply the missing bit.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{proof}

We begin with the forward direction. Suppose the lines $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ are concurrent at an interior point $X$.
We will show $\dfrac{AF}{FB}\cdot\dfrac{BD}{CD}\cdot\dfrac{CE}{AE} = 1$.
Denote the area of $\triangle XYZ$ by $[XYZ]$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.4, font=\small\sffamily, line join=round]
    \coordinate (A) at (-2, 2);
    \coordinate (B) at (-2, 0);
    \coordinate (C) at (2, 1.5);
    \coordinate (D) at (-0.4, 0.6);
    \coordinate (E) at (0.4, 1.7);
    \coordinate (F) at (-2, 0.615);
    \coordinate (X) at (-0.74, 0.89);

    % Shade the three inner triangles (fills first, behind everything)
    \fill[yellow!25] (A) -- (B) -- (X) -- cycle;
    \fill[green!15]  (B) -- (C) -- (X) -- cycle;
    \fill[blue!15]   (A) -- (C) -- (X) -- cycle;

    % Main triangle
    \draw[thick] (A) -- (B) -- (C) -- cycle;

    % Three concurrent cevians in distinct colors
    \draw[thick, blue!70!black]  (A) -- (D);
    \draw[thick, red!70!black]   (B) -- (E);
    \draw[thick, green!60!black] (C) -- (F);

    % Area labels at centroids of each inner triangle
    \node[font=\scriptsize] at (-1.58, 0.96) {$[ABX]$};
    \node[font=\scriptsize] at (-0.25, 0.75) {$[BCX]$};
    \node[font=\scriptsize] at (-0.25, 1.46) {$[ACX]$};

    % Point labels
    \fill[black] (A) circle (2pt) node[above]      {$A$};
    \fill[black] (B) circle (2pt) node[below left]  {$B$};
    \fill[black] (C) circle (2pt) node[right]        {$C$};
    \fill[black] (D) circle (2pt) node[below right]  {$D$};
    \fill[black] (E) circle (2pt) node[above]        {$E$};
    \fill[black] (F) circle (2pt) node[left]         {$F$};
    \fill[black] (X) circle (2pt) node[above]        {$X$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\textbf{Claim 1:} $\dfrac{BD}{CD} = \dfrac{[ABX]}{[ACX]}$.

Triangles $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ACD$ share the altitude from $A$ to line $BC$, so
\[
    \frac{[ABD]}{[ACD]} = \frac{BD}{CD}.
\]
Triangles $\triangle XBD$ and $\triangle XCD$ share the altitude from $X$ to line $BC$, so
\[
    \frac{[XBD]}{[XCD]} = \frac{BD}{CD}.
\]
Since $X$ lies on segment $\overline{AD}$, we have $[ABD] = [ABX] + [XBD]$ and $[ACD] = [ACX] + [XCD]$. Subtracting:
\[
    \frac{BD}{CD} = \frac{[ABD] - [XBD]}{[ACD] - [XCD]} = \frac{[ABX]}{[ACX]}.
\]

\textbf{Claim 2:} $\dfrac{CE}{AE} = \dfrac{[BCX]}{[ABX]}$.

Applying the same argument to the line $\overline{BE}$, with $X$ on segment $\overline{BE}$ and $E$ on $\overline{CA}$: triangles $\triangle BCE$ and $\triangle ABE$ share the altitude from $B$ to $CA$, while $\triangle XCE$ and $\triangle XAE$ share the altitude from $X$ to $CA$. Since $[BCE] = [BCX] + [XCE]$ and $[ABE] = [ABX] + [XAE]$:
\[
    \frac{CE}{AE} = \frac{[BCE] - [XCE]}{[ABE] - [XAE]} = \frac{[BCX]}{[ABX]}.
\]

\textbf{Claim 3:} $\dfrac{AF}{FB} = \dfrac{[ACX]}{[BCX]}$.

Applying the same argument to the line $\overline{CF}$, with $X$ on segment $\overline{CF}$ and $F$ on $\overline{AB}$: triangles $\triangle ACF$ and $\triangle BCF$ share the altitude from $C$ to $AB$, while $\triangle XAF$ and $\triangle XBF$ share the altitude from $X$ to $AB$. Since $[ACF] = [ACX] + [XAF]$ and $[BCF] = [BCX] + [XBF]$:
\[
    \frac{AF}{FB} = \frac{[ACF] - [XAF]}{[BCF] - [XBF]} = \frac{[ACX]}{[BCX]}.
\]

Multiplying the three claims, the areas telescope:
\[
    \frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{CD}\cdot\frac{CE}{AE}
    \;=\; \frac{[ACX]}{[BCX]}\cdot\frac{[ABX]}{[ACX]}\cdot\frac{[BCX]}{[ABX]} \;=\; 1.\qedhere
\]

The proof above covers the interior case using ordinary areas.  A uniform proof
for points on extensions uses \emph{signed} areas; ordinary areas cannot simply
be subtracted without checking the point order.  The next diagram illustrates
one exterior order.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9, font=\small\sffamily, line join=round]
    \coordinate (A) at (-2, 2);
    \coordinate (B) at (-2, 0);
    \coordinate (C) at (2, 1.5);
    \coordinate (D) at (-2.8, -0.3);    % line BC extended, left of B
    \coordinate (E) at (0.4, 1.7);      % segment CA (between C and A)
    \coordinate (F) at (-2, -0.25);     % line AB extended, below B
    \coordinate (X) at (-2.92, -0.65);  % concurrency point, outside ABC

    % Triangle ABC
    \draw[thick] (A) -- (B) -- (C) -- cycle;

    % Dashed extensions of BC past B to D, and of AB past B to F
    \draw[thick] (B) -- (D);
    \draw[thick] (B) -- (F);

    % Three cevian lines, all meeting at X outside the triangle
    \draw[thick, blue!70!black]  (A) -- (X);   % through D on BC extended
    \draw[thick, red!70!black]   (E) -- (X);   % through B
    \draw[thick, green!60!black] (C) -- (X);   % through F on AB extended

    % Points
    \fill[black] (A) circle (2pt) node[left]        {$A$};
    \fill[black] (B) circle (2pt) node[right]        {$B$};
    \fill[black] (C) circle (2pt) node[right]        {$C$};
    \fill[black] (D) circle (2pt) node[left]   {$D$};
    \fill[black] (E) circle (2pt) node[above right]  {$E$};
    \fill[black] (F) circle (2pt) node[below]         {$F$};
    \fill[black] (X) circle (2pt) node[below]        {$X$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Here $D$ lies on the extension of $\overline{BC}$ beyond $B$, $F$ lies on the extension of $\overline{AB}$ beyond $B$, and $X$ lies outside $\triangle ABC$. In this configuration $X$ lies beyond $D$ on line $AD$ (order: $A$--$D$--$X$), so the area decomposition in each claim \emph{adds} instead of subtracts. For Claim~1, since $[ABX] = [ABD]+[XBD]$ and $[ACX]=[ACD]+[XCD]$:
\[
    \frac{BD}{CD} = r
    \;\Longrightarrow\;
    \frac{[ABX]}{[ACX]} = \frac{[ABD]+[XBD]}{[ACD]+[XCD]}
    = \frac{r\,[ACD]+r\,[XCD]}{[ACD]+[XCD]} = r,
\]
and analogous additions hold in Claims~2 and~3. The telescoping product is identical, so $\frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{CD}\cdot\frac{CE}{AE}=1$ in both cases.

Now for the converse. The proof is analogous to the converse of Menelaus's theorem. In particular, if $BE$ and $CF$ intersect at the point $X$,
Then if we draw the line from $AX$ and say it intersects at the point $D'$ on the line $BC$. The claim will be that $D=D'$. From the forward direction of 
Ceva's theorem since the lines are concurrent, we have that 
\[
\dfrac{AF}{FB}\cdot\dfrac{BD'}{CD'}\cdot \dfrac{CE}{AE} = 1.
\]
Cancelling the two common nonzero ratios gives
\[
\dfrac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} =
\dfrac{\overline{BD'}}{\overline{D'C}}.
\]
The directed ratio is injective along line $BC$, so $D=D'$.
\end{proof}


\section{Pappus's Theorem}\label{sec: Pappus}

We now prove Pappus's Hexagon Theorem. 

\begin{thm}[Pappus's Hexagon Theorem]
Suppose $A,E,C$ lie on one line and $D,B,F$ on another. Define
$X=AB\cap DE$, $Y=CD\cap FA$, and $Z=BC\cap EF$, assuming these intersections
are finite and distinct. Then $X,Y,Z$ are collinear. In parser notation the
incidence conclusion is \verb|coll X Y Z|.
\end{thm}

\begin{lecturebox}{Solver lens: Pappus}

directly. The proof below instead uses repeated Menelaus applications and
cancellation, which exposes what the small solver lacks: directed signs,
reliable intersection preconditions, and treatment of parallel points at
infinity. It is therefore a useful stress test for the construction library
and dependency graph even when it lies beyond the core DD+AR vocabulary.
\end{lecturebox}

    \[ \frac{PE}{EQ} \cdot \frac{QF}{FR} \cdot \frac{RZ}{ZP} = -1 \]

    \[ \frac{PX}{XQ} \cdot \frac{QA}{AR} \cdot \frac{RB}{BP} = -1 \]

To isolate these terms, we multiply equations 3, 4, and 5 together, and then multiply by the inverses of equations 1 and 2:

\[
\left( \frac{PD}{DQ} \cdot \frac{QY}{YR}\cdot  \frac{RC}{CP} \right) \left( \frac{PE}{EQ}\cdot \frac{QF}{FR} \cdot \frac{RZ}{ZP} \right) \left( \frac{PX}{XQ} \cdot \frac{QA}{AR}\cdot  \frac{RB}{BP} \right) \left( \frac{EQ}{PE}\cdot \frac{AR}{QA}\cdot \frac{CP}{RC} \right) \left( \frac{DQ}{PD}\cdot \frac{FR}{QF} \cdot\frac{BP}{RB} \right) = -1
\]

Regrouping the fractions to cancel corresponding terms:

\[
\left( \frac{PD}{DQ}\cdot \frac{DQ}{PD} \right) \left( \frac{RC}{CP}\cdot \frac{CP}{RC} \right) \left( \frac{PE}{EQ}\cdot \frac{EQ}{PE} \right) \left( \frac{QF}{FR}\cdot \frac{FR}{QF} \right) \left( \frac{QA}{AR}\cdot \frac{AR}{QA} \right) \left( \frac{RB}{BP}\cdot \frac{BP}{RB} \right) \left( \frac{PX}{XQ} \cdot \frac{QY}{YR} \cdot \frac{RZ}{ZP} \right) = -1
\]

All of the intermediate ratios cancel perfectly to $1$, leaving only:

\[
\frac{PX}{XQ} \cdot \frac{QY}{YR} \cdot \frac{RZ}{ZP} = -1
\]

By the converse of Menelaus's Theorem, because the product of the directed ratios along the sides of $\triangle PQR$ equals $-1$, the points $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ must be collinear.
\end{proof}

    degenerate cases require a projective argument or a limiting argument.
\end{remark}

\section{Pascal's Theorem}\label{sec: Pascal}

We now prove Pascal's Hexagon Theorem.

\begin{thm}[Pascal's Hexagon Theorem]
Assume that a hexagon $ABCDEF$ is inscribed in a circle. Define
$X=CD\cap FA$, $Y=AB\cap DE$, and $Z=EF\cap BC$, whenever these intersections
are finite. Then \verb|coll X Y Z|.
\end{thm}

\begin{lecturebox}{Solver lens: Pascal}
Pascal combines circle incidence, line intersections, power-of-a-point
identities, Menelaus, and a final collinearity conclusion. Some nonzero
length-product cancellations fit the log-ratio table, but the theorem still
needs DD to justify the cyclic and secant configurations and needs directed
sign information absent from the core AR. A robust implementation must also
separate the finite general-position case from parallel or coincident
intersections rather than trusting one successful diagram.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{proof}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item (Red) Line $DXC$ intersecting $\triangle GHI$:
    \[ \frac{GC}{CI} \cdot \frac{ID}{DH} \cdot \frac{HX}{XG} = -1 \]
    
    \item (Orange) Line $AYB$ intersecting $\triangle GHI$:
    \[ \frac{HA}{AG} \cdot \frac{GB}{BI} \cdot \frac{IY}{YH} = -1 \]
    
    \item (Green) Line $EZF$ intersecting $\triangle GHI$:
    \[ \frac{HF}{FG} \cdot \frac{GZ}{ZI} \cdot \frac{IE}{EH} = -1 \]
\end{enumerate}

We can see that the ratios $\frac{HX}{XG}$, $\frac{IY}{YH}$, and $\frac{GZ}{ZI}$ are the exact three components we need to apply the converse of Menelaus's theorem for the line $XYZ$ with respect to $\triangle GHI$. 

To isolate these terms, we multiply all three equations together. Grouping the intersections $G$, $H$, and $I$ with their corresponding intersecting chords on the circle yields the following equations by the Power of a Point (Intersecting Chords) Theorem:

\begin{align*}
    \frac{DI \cdot IE}{BI \cdot IC} &= 1 \\
    \frac{BG \cdot GC}{AG \cdot GF} &= 1 \\
    \frac{AH \cdot HF}{DH \cdot HE} &= 1
\end{align*}

Thus, when we multiply the three Menelaus equations together and apply these power of a point identities, the lengths of the circle's chords cancel out perfectly to $1$:

\[
\left( \frac{GC \cdot GB}{AG \cdot FG} \right) \left( \frac{HA \cdot HF}{DH \cdot EH} \right) \left( \frac{ID \cdot IE}{CI \cdot BI} \right) \left( \frac{HX}{XG} \cdot \frac{IY}{YH} \cdot \frac{GZ}{ZI} \right) = -1
\]

\[
(1) \cdot (1) \cdot (1) \cdot \left( \frac{HX}{XG} \cdot \frac{IY}{YH} \cdot \frac{GZ}{ZI} \right) = -1
\]

This simplifies to:
\[
\frac{HX}{XG} \cdot \frac{IY}{YH} \cdot \frac{GZ}{ZI} = -1
\]

By the converse to Menelaus's theorem, because the product of the directed ratios along the sides of $\triangle GHI$ equals $-1$, we conclude that the points $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ must be collinear.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    The proof assumes that $AF$, $BC$, and $DE$ are pairwise nonparallel and
    nonconcurrent, so that $GHI$ is a nondegenerate triangle. Parallel or
    degenerate cases require a projective or limiting argument.
\end{remark}

\section{Brianchon's Theorem}\label{sec: Brianchon}

We will now discuss Brianchon's theorem. 

\begin{thm}[Brianchon's theorem]
    Let $ABCDEF$ be a convex hexagon circumscribed about a conic (in
    particular, about a circle). Projectively, $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$ are
    concurrent. In an affine chart this means that they meet in one finite
    point or are all parallel.
\end{thm}

\begin{lecturebox}{Solver lens: Brianchon}
Brianchon is projectively dual to Pascal. For a course solver it tests a
different interface: tangency predicates and tangent constructions must be
represented explicitly, and the conclusion is three-line concurrency rather
than collinearity. In an affine point-only state, finite concurrency can be
certified by an intersection point incident with all three diagonals. The
all-parallel case represents concurrency at infinity and is outside that
finite representation unless the system adds projective points or a separate
parallel-case theorem.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{proof}


    Let the hexagon $ABCDEF$ be circumscribed around a circle $\omega$ with center $O$ (black circle). Let the points of tangency of $\omega$ with the sides $AB$, $BC$, $CD$, $DE$, $EF$, and $FA$ be $T_1$, $T_2$, $T_3$, $T_4$, $T_5$, and $T_6$ respectively.

Because the sides of the hexagon are tangent to the inscribed circle, the tangent segments drawn from each vertex to the incircle are equal in length. We denote these lengths as:
\begin{align*}
    a &= AT_1 = AT_6 \\
    b &= BT_1 = BT_2 \\
    c &= CT_2 = CT_3 \\
    d &= DT_3 = DT_4 \\
    e &= ET_4 = ET_5 \\
    f &= FT_5 = FT_6
\end{align*}

To construct our three specific circles, choose
$0<k<\min\{b,d,f\}$, avoiding the finitely many degenerate choices, and shift
the tangency points along the oriented sides. We plot six new points, $P_1$
through $P_6$ (labeled in orange), by alternately adding and subtracting $k$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item On side $AB$, plot $P_1$ such that $AP_1 = a + k$ and $BP_1 = b - k$.
    \item On side $BC$, plot $P_2$ such that $BP_2 = b - k$ and $CP_2 = c + k$.
    \item On side $CD$, plot $P_3$ such that $CP_3 = c + k$ and $DP_3 = d - k$.
    \item On side $DE$, plot $P_4$ such that $DP_4 = d - k$ and $EP_4 = e + k$.
    \item On side $EF$, plot $P_5$ such that $EP_5 = e + k$ and $FP_5 = f - k$.
    \item On side $FA$, plot $P_6$ such that $FP_6 = f - k$ and $AP_6 = a + k$.
\end{itemize}

By alternating the arithmetic sign of the shift $k$, the distances from each vertex to its two adjacent offset points remain perfectly equal: 
\[ AP_1 = AP_6, \quad BP_1 = BP_2, \quad CP_2 = CP_3, \quad DP_3 = DP_4, \quad EP_4 = EP_5, \quad FP_5 = FP_6 \]

We now define three auxiliary circles $C_1$ (blue), $C_2$ (green), and $C_3$ (red) that are tangent to opposite sides of the hexagon at these new shifted points:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Let $C_1$ be the (blue) circle tangent to $BC$ at $P_2$ and tangent to $EF$ at $P_5$.
    \item Let $C_2$ be the (green) circle tangent to $CD$ at $P_3$ and tangent to $FA$ at $P_6$.
    \item Let $C_3$ be the (red) circle tangent to $AB$ at $P_1$ and tangent to $DE$ at $P_4$.
\end{itemize}

In general position, the reason these circles exist is the uniform directed
shift of the $T_i$. Given two intersecting lines and one point on each, a
circle tangent to both lines at the specified points exists exactly when the
two directed distances from the intersection agree.
For example, we see that the circle $C_1$ exists as follows. We want $C_1$ to be tangent to $BC$ and $EF$. Then if we let $R$ be the point of intersection of $BC$ and $EF$. Then since the circle
$\omega$ is tangent to $BC$ and $EF$ at $T_2$ and $T_5$. We know that the $\overline{RT_2}=\overline{RT_5}$. Then we will have that $\overline{RP_2}=\overline{RP_5}$ since we will just be adjusting
the distance by a factor of $k$ from the intersection point. 

We now find the radical axes for these three pairs of circles. For each vertex
below, the relevant segment is tangent to the corresponding auxiliary circle,
so its squared length equals the power.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Circles $C_2$ and $C_3$:} 
    The power of vertex $A$ to $C_3$ (red) is $AP_1^2$, and its power to $C_2$ (green) is $AP_6^2$. Since $AP_1 = AP_6$, vertex $A$ has equal power to both circles. Similarly, the power of vertex $D$ to $C_3$ (red) is $DP_4^2$, and its power to $C_2$ (green) is $DP_3^2$. Since $DP_3 = DP_4$, vertex $D$ also has equal power to both circles. Therefore, the line connecting them, the diagonal \textbf{$AD$}, is the radical axis of $C_2$ and $C_3$.

    \item \textbf{Circles $C_1$ and $C_3$:} 
    By the exact same logic, $BP_1 = BP_2$ and $EP_4 = EP_5$, meaning vertices $B$ and $E$ have equal power to $C_1$ (blue) and $C_3$ (red). Therefore, the diagonal \textbf{$BE$} is the radical axis of $C_1$ and $C_3$.

    \item \textbf{Circles $C_1$ and $C_2$:}
    Vertices $C$ and $F$ have equal power to $C_1$ (blue) and $C_2$ (green) because $CP_2 = CP_3$ and $FP_5 = FP_6$. Therefore, the diagonal \textbf{$CF$} is the radical axis of $C_1$ and $C_2$.
\end{enumerate}

By the Radical Center Theorem, the three pairwise radical axes are concurrent
or all parallel. Since they are exactly $AD$, $BE$, and $CF$, this proves the
affine form stated above; projectively the parallel case is concurrency at
infinity.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{\texttt{rules.txt}: the deduction rules used by DD}

Each line of \verb|rules.txt| is one DD rule:
\[
\texttt{premises => conclusion}.
\]
The parser assigns the 43 current rules positional identifiers
\verb|r00| through \verb|r42|; the descriptive names below are teaching
labels, not comments stored in the file.  These identifiers are tied to the
current file order, so inserting or reordering a rule can change later
identifiers. DD repeatedly looks for rules whose
premises already hold and then adds the conclusion as a new fact.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{equal-inscribed-angles-same-chord}
          \begin{center}
          \codeseg{cyclic A B P Q}
          \(\Longrightarrow\)
          \codeseg{eqangle P A P B Q A Q B}
          \end{center}
          In words: if $A,B,P,Q$ lie on a common circle, then the angle that
          chord $AB$ subtends at $P$ equals the angle it subtends at $Q$. This
          is exactly the Inscribed Angle Theorem.
    \item \emph{SSS-congruence}
          \begin{center}
          \codeseg{cong A B P Q}, \codeseg{cong B C Q R}\\
          \codeseg{cong C A R P}, \codeseg{ncoll A B C}
          \(\Longrightarrow\)
          \codeseg{contri* A B C P Q R}
          \end{center}
          In words: if the three sides of triangle
          $ABC$ are respectively congruent to the three sides of triangle
          $PQR$ (and $A,B,C$ are not collinear, i.e. actually form a
          triangle), then the two triangles are congruent. This is the SSS
          congruence criterion from classical geometry, written here as a
          single mechanical rule DD can apply.
    \item \emph{parallel-from-double-perpendiculars}
          \begin{center}
          \codeseg{perp A B C D}, \codeseg{perp C D E F}\\
          \codeseg{ncoll A B E}
          \(\Longrightarrow\)
          \codeseg{para A B E F}
          \end{center}
          In words: if line
          $AB$ is perpendicular to $CD$, and $CD$ is also perpendicular to
          $EF$, then $AB$ is parallel to $EF$ - two lines perpendicular to the
          same line are parallel to each other.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texttt{defs.txt}: the construction actions used to state problems}

Every construction you can write when formalizing a problem (recall
\verb|on_tline|, \verb|midp|, \verb|on_circle|, etc. from \Cref{sec: Intro})
is defined by a six-line block in \verb|defs.txt|. For example:
\begin{lstlisting}
midpoint x a b
x : a b
a b = diff a b
x : coll x a b, cong x a x b
midp a b

\end{lstlisting}
The six lines have fixed roles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \verb|midpoint x a b| is the signature: construct a new point
          \verb|x| from existing points \verb|a| and \verb|b|.
    \item \verb|x : a b| records that \verb|x| depends on \verb|a| and
          \verb|b|.
    \item \verb|a b = diff a b| is a non-degeneracy condition: \verb|a| and
          \verb|b| must be distinct.
    \item \verb|x : coll x a b, cong x a x b| gives the symbolic facts added
          to the proof state. A midpoint lies on line $AB$ and satisfies
          $XA=XB$.
    \item \verb|midp a b| names the numeric drawing routine in
          \verb|numericals.py|.
    \item The blank line separates this definition from the next one.
\end{enumerate}
The key distinction is between line 4 and line 5. Line 4 is what the prover is
allowed to use in a proof. Line 5 is only how the program draws one generic
diagram.

A second, shorter example, \verb|on_circle|:
\begin{lstlisting}
on_circle x o a
x : x o a
o a = diff o a
x : cong o x o a
circle o o a

\end{lstlisting}
This constructs a new point \verb|x| lying on the circle centered at
\verb|o| through \verb|a|; the only symbolic fact recorded is
\verb|cong o x o a| (\verb|x| is the same distance from \verb|o| as \verb|a|
is), which is exactly what it means for \verb|x| to lie on that circle.

\subsection{A Closer Look at \texttt{dd.py}}\label{sec: dd.py}

\begin{lecturebox}{Big idea}
DD is theorem pattern matching.
\end{lecturebox}

A line in \verb|rules.txt| is not just one theorem about one particular
diagram. It is a reusable pattern. For example, the rule
\begin{center}
\codeseg{cyclic A B P Q}
\(\Longrightarrow\)
\codeseg{eqangle P A P B Q A Q B}
\end{center}
says: whenever the current problem contains four actual points playing the
roles of \(A,B,P,Q\), and those four points are known to be cyclic, DD may
add the corresponding equal-angle statement. Thus \verb|dd.py| is mainly a
pattern matcher. It searches the current proof graph for all ways to replace
the letters \(A,B,P,Q,\ldots\) in a rule by the actual points in the problem.

There are two ways this matching is done:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Optimized matching functions (currently 26 entries in
          \verb|BUILT_IN_FNS|) exploit the proof graph's internal indices
          ---for example, points
          already grouped by which \emph{direction} or \emph{circle} they lie
          on - to jump straight to plausible matches instead of searching
          blindly. \verb|match_cyclic_eqangle|, implementing the Inscribed
          Angle Theorem rule from \Cref{sec: Data Directory}, is one such
          function.
    \item Every other rule falls back to \verb|match_generic|, a small
          general-purpose constraint solver: it looks up all currently known
          instances of each premise predicate (e.g. every known \verb|coll|
          triple), then recursively tries to combine them into one consistent
          assignment of points to variables, backtracking whenever two
          premises disagree about what a shared variable should be.
\end{itemize}

Either way, the output is the same: a dictionary assigning the rule's
variables to actual points. Once a rule has been matched,
\verb|bfs_one_level| does three things:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Match.} Find every match of every rule against the current
          proof state, as just described.
    \item \textbf{Justify.} For each match, double check that each premise is
          actually already known (not just numerically true), and record
          \emph{why} - which earlier facts justify it - in a
          \verb|Dependency| object. This bookkeeping is exactly what allows
          \verb|trace_back.py| to later reconstruct a dependency-backed
          reduced proof.
    \item \textbf{Add.} Add the rule's conclusion to the proof graph as a new
          fact (skipping it if already known) and register any corresponding
          algebraic relation.
\end{enumerate}
The controller in \verb|ddar.py| runs DD to its current fixed point (or to the
goal), asks AR for newly justified consequences, inserts those facts, and then
resumes DD.  Thus information flows in both directions, although AR is not
fully re-run after each individual DD match. DD itself explores consequences
breadth-first. The command-line \verb|--search_depth| is unrelated to these DD
levels: it bounds LM auxiliary-construction rounds.

What different roles do \verb|defs.txt| and \verb|rules.txt| play? After a DD
rule has been matched, what must happen before its conclusion may enter the
proof graph? Why is a numerically plausible match insufficient?
\end{conceptcheck}

\subsection{A Closer Look at \texttt{ar.py}}\label{sec: ar.py}

\begin{lecturebox}{Big idea}
AR turns geometry into linear equations.
\end{lecturebox}

DD is good at applying named theorems. AR is good at chasing many equalities
at once. Whenever the graph learns a new angle, ratio, or distance fact, AR
translates it into a linear equation. Two quantities are then equal exactly
when their algebraic expressions become identical. This is also a major speed
gain: instead of rediscovering equality consequences by many small theorem
applications, AR lets Gaussian elimination propagate whole families of
equalities in one table.

There are three such tables, matching the three private attributes
\verb|atable|, \verb|rtable|, \verb|dtable| on the proof graph in
\verb|graph.py|:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \verb|AngleTable|: one variable per line \emph{direction}, namely
          the value $d(\cdot)$ from \Cref{sec: d(AB)}, taken modulo $\pi$
          (\verb|para|, \verb|perp|, and \verb|eqangle| facts all become
          linear equations among these directions). For instance,
          \verb|eqangle P A P B Q A Q B| becomes, up to the modulo-$\pi$
          convention,
          \[
          d(PA)-d(PB)=d(QA)-d(QB).
          \]
          Similarly, \verb|para A B C D| says \(d(AB)-d(CD)=0\), while
          \verb|perp A B C D| says \(d(AB)-d(CD)=\pi/2\).

          \begin{lecturebox}{Remark: why modulo \(\pi\)?}
          A line, unlike a ray, has no preferred direction. Walking along
          \(AB\) toward \(A\) or toward \(B\) gives the same line. Therefore
          \(d(AB)\) is only defined up to an added multiple of \(\pi\). The
          \verb|AngleTable| handles this by reducing the coefficient of
          \verb|pi| modulo \(1\) whenever it checks whether an expression is
          zero:
          \begin{lstlisting}
>>> tb.modulo({'pi': -1})
{}
>>> tb.modulo({'pi': Fraction(1, 2)})
{'pi': Fraction(1, 2)}
\end{lstlisting}
          A whole multiple of \(\pi\) disappears, while a genuine right angle
          \(\pi/2\) remains. This does discard orientation: \(d(AB)\) does
          not know which side of a line a point lies on, which is why
          orientation-sensitive triangle rules need an additional orientation
          check. In this repository that check is the numerical helper
          \verb|same_clock|, rather than a parser-level predicate. The diagram
          is used to choose the appropriate triangle correspondence.
          \end{lecturebox}
    \item \verb|RatioTable|: one variable per segment \emph{length}, but
          stored as its \emph{logarithm}. Ratio facts such as
          $a/b = c/d$ are multiplicative, not linear - but
          $\log a - \log b = \log c - \log d$ is linear, so taking logarithms
          turns \verb|cong| and \verb|eqratio| facts into ordinary linear
          equations too.
    \item \verb|DistanceTable|: one variable per (point, line) pair, namely
          that point's $1$-dimensional coordinate along that specific line.
          This is what lets AR do intercept-theorem-style reasoning about
          several congruent or proportional segments lying on a common line.
          If \(x_\ell(P)\) denotes the coordinate of point \(P\) along line
          \(\ell\), then a congruence such as \(AB=CD\), with \(A,B\) on
          line \(\ell\) and \(C,D\) on line \(m\), becomes
          \[
          x_\ell(A)-x_\ell(B)=x_m(C)-x_m(D),
          \]
          after choosing consistent orientations from the numeric diagram.
\end{itemize}

All three are built on the same generic \verb|Table| class, which keeps a
dictionary \verb|v2e| ("variable to expression") expressing every known
quantity as an affine combination of a \emph{shrinking} set of free
variables, plus a special constant (\verb|pi| for \verb|AngleTable|, \verb|1|
for the other two). Adding a new equality is one step of Gaussian
elimination: if it only involves quantities already expressed in terms of
the current free variables, one of those gets eliminated and re-expressed in
terms of the rest (a pivot); if it mentions something new, that quantity
simply gets expressed in terms of what is already known. Finding
\emph{every} new fact is then just comparing every pair of expressions and
checking whether their difference collapses to a constant.

\begin{lecturebox}{Remark: where is the Gaussian elimination?}
AR does not first build one large matrix and then run Gaussian elimination
from scratch. Instead, the table is kept in a reduced form as the proof
develops. Each new geometric fact contributes one linear equation, and
\verb|add_expr| immediately performs the corresponding pivot: it solves for
one variable in terms of the remaining free variables and substitutes that
expression throughout \verb|v2e|. This is why AR is fast. Equality
consequences are propagated by algebraic normalization, rather than by many
separate theorem applications. The separate matrix stored by \verb|register|
is mainly for proof traceback: later, a small linear program asks which input
facts were responsible for a derived equality.
\end{lecturebox}

\examplepart{Setting up the table}
Rather than running full DD+AR, the test
builds a bare \verb|AngleTable| directly and feeds it three facts by hand,
written using the directions $d(AC)$, $d(CD)$, $d(BC)$, $d(CE)$ from
\Cref{sec: d(AB)}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{fact1}: $CD$ bisects $\angle ACB$, i.e. the angle from $AC$ to
          $CD$ equals the angle from $CD$ to $BC$.
    \item \emph{fact2}: $CE$ is the external bisector of $\angle ACB$.
    \item \emph{fact3} (a distractor, deliberately unrelated to the
          perpendicularity claim): some other equal-angle fact about line
          $AB$, added only to confirm that AR's traceback correctly ignores
          it.
\end{itemize}

\examplepart{Watching the elimination happen, one fact at a time}
This is the
same incremental Gaussian elimination described above, but now traced
through concretely. Before any facts are added, the table only knows about
the constant:
\begin{lstlisting}
v2e:  pi = pi
\end{lstlisting}
After \emph{fact1} alone, $d(AC)$ is no longer free: the table has pivoted
and re-expressed it in terms of $d(BC)$ and $d(CD)$, which remain free
because nothing yet constrains them relative to each other:
\begin{lstlisting}
v2e:  d(ac) = -d(bc) + 2*d(cd)
      d(bc) = d(bc)
      d(cd) = d(cd)
      pi    = pi
\end{lstlisting}
This already says something: $d(AC) = 2d(CD) - d(BC)$, i.e.
$d(CD) = \tfrac12(d(AC)+d(BC))$ - a bisector's direction really is the
average of the two directions it bisects, and the table now carries that
fact for every later computation involving $d(AC)$. After \emph{fact2} is
also added, $d(CE)$ becomes expressible too, this time in terms of $d(CD)$
alone plus a multiple of $\pi$:
\begin{lstlisting}
v2e:  d(ac) = -d(bc) + 2*d(cd)
      d(bc) = d(bc)
      d(cd) = d(cd)
      d(ce) = d(cd) + -1/2*pi
      pi    = pi
\end{lstlisting}
This is already the answer, sitting in the table after only two facts: $d(CE)$
and $d(CD)$ differ by the constant $-\frac12\pi$. Adding the distractor
\emph{fact3} pivots $d(AB)$ into the table as well, but - this is the point
of including it - it does not touch the already-settled rows for $d(CD)$ or
$d(CE)$ at all:
\begin{lstlisting}
AR Table AngleTable (const=pi)
variables=6 eqs=12 groups=0
v2e (variable expansions):
  d(ab) = 3*d(bc) + -2*d(cd) + -pi
  d(ac) = -d(bc) + 2*d(cd)
  d(bc) = d(bc)
  d(cd) = d(cd)
  d(ce) = d(cd) + -1/2*pi
  pi = pi
\end{lstlisting}
The same final state, displayed as an explicit matrix (rows = every
direction seen so far, columns = the three basis quantities the system has
settled on - the constant \verb|pi|, and the two directions \verb|d(bc)| and
\verb|d(cd)| that turned out to be independent of everything else):
\begin{lstlisting}
var       pi        d(bc)     d(cd)
----------------------------------------
d(ab)     -1/1      3/1       -2/1
d(ac)     .         -1/1      2/1
d(bc)     .         1/1       .
d(cd)     .         .         1/1
d(ce)     -1/2      .         1/1
pi        1/1       .         .
\end{lstlisting}
Every row is to be read as "(row variable) = (entry under \texttt{pi})$\cdot
\pi$ + (entry under \texttt{d(bc)})$\cdot d(bc)$ + (entry under
\texttt{d(cd)})$\cdot d(cd)$", a dot meaning a zero coefficient; e.g. the
\verb|d(ce)| row reads $d(CE) = -\frac12 \pi + d(CD)$, matching the
\verb|v2e| line above it. Comparing the rows for \verb|d(cd)| and
\verb|d(ce)|: their difference is $d(CD) - d(CE) = \frac12\pi$, a
\emph{constant}, independent of the two remaining free variables
\verb|d(bc)|, \verb|d(cd)| - whatever the actual triangle looks like, this
difference never changes. A constant difference of exactly $\pi/2$ between
two directions is precisely what it means for two lines to be perpendicular,
so AR has discovered $CD \perp CE$ purely by linear algebra, without ever
consulting a rule about bisectors.

\examplepart{Finding out \emph{why}}
The table above answers \emph{whether} a
fact is true, but proof output also needs to know \emph{which} of the
original input facts were actually responsible - exactly the role
\verb|Dependency| objects play for DD in \Cref{sec: dd.py}. AR answers this
with a second matrix, completely separate from \verb|v2e|, by solving a
small linear program over it. \Cref{sec: AR LP} works through exactly how
this matrix is built and solved, first on a simple non-geometric example and
then on this very problem.

\end{conceptcheck}

\subsection{Optional: Linear Programming and the \emph{Why} Matrix}\label{sec: AR LP}

\begin{lecturebox}{Optional deep dive}
This subsection first introduces linear programming as a mathematical topic
in its own right. Only after that do we return to AR and explain why a linear
program appears in proof traceback.
\end{lecturebox}

\examplepart{What is a linear program?}
A \emph{linear program} is an optimization problem in which both the
quantity being optimized and the constraints are linear. In one common
form, we choose a vector \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in \mathbb{R}^n\) and solve
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{minimize}   & c^\top x \\[2mm]
\text{subject to} & Ax \leq b,\\
                  & x\geq 0.
\end{array}
\]
The vector \(c\) gives the linear objective function, the inequalities
\(Ax\leq b\) describe the feasible region, and the condition \(x\geq0\)
usually means that the variables represent amounts of something: bags,
hours, weights, probabilities, or, later, weights assigned to proof facts.

Geometrically, the feasible region is an intersection of half-spaces, hence
a convex polygon in two variables, a convex polyhedron in three variables,
and a convex polytope in higher dimensions (possibly unbounded). The key
feature is that a linear objective has no hidden curvature. If an optimum
exists, one can find an optimum at a vertex of the feasible region.

\examplepart{Equality form}
The version used by \verb|scipy.optimize.linprog| in \verb|ar.py| is closer
to
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{minimize}   & c^\top x \\[2mm]
\text{subject to} & A_{\mathrm{eq}}x=b_{\mathrm{eq}},\\
                  & x\geq0.
\end{array}
\]
This is called an \emph{equality-constrained} or \emph{standard-form}
linear program. Inequalities can be converted into equalities by adding
slack or surplus variables. For example, \(2p+q\geq5\) is equivalent to
\[
2p+q-s=5,\qquad s\geq0,
\]
where \(s\) records the amount by which the left-hand side exceeds the
minimum requirement.

\examplepart{A farmer example}
A farmer can buy two fertilizer mixes:
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc|c}
       & \text{nitrogen} & \text{phosphorus} & \text{cost} \\
\hline
P      & 2              & 1                 & 3 \\
Q      & 1              & 3                 & 2
\end{array}
\]
The field needs at least \(5\) units of nitrogen and at least \(5\) units of
phosphorus. If \(p,q\geq0\) are the numbers of bags of \(P\) and \(Q\), the
problem is
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{minimize}   & 3p+2q \\[1mm]
\text{subject to} & 2p+q\geq5,\\
                  & p+3q\geq5,\\
                  & p,q\geq0.
\end{array}
\]
The two nutrient constraints meet at
\[
2p+q=5,\qquad p+3q=5,
\]
which gives \(p=2\), \(q=1\). The cost is \(3\cdot2+2\cdot1=8\).

Why is this optimal? The feasible region lies above both lines
\(2p+q=5\) and \(p+3q=5\) in the first quadrant. Moving the objective line
\(3p+2q=\text{constant}\) downward, the first feasible point it touches is
the intersection \((2,1)\). Other vertices are more expensive: using only
\(P\) requires \(p=5\), cost \(15\); using only \(Q\) requires \(q=5\), cost
\(10\). Thus the best solution is two bags of \(P\) and one bag of \(Q\).

A third mix \(R\) containing only potassium and sulfur would receive weight
\(0\) in an optimal solution, because it helps none of the two constraints
we care about while increasing the cost. This simple observation is the
part that will matter for AR: a linear program can automatically ignore
irrelevant ingredients.

\examplepart{Returning to AR}
In AR, the "ingredients" are not bags of fertilizer but previously known
linear facts. The target is not a nutrient requirement but a new equality
whose proof we want to explain. The question becomes:
\[
\text{Can the target equality be obtained as a linear combination of the
already registered facts?}
\]
This question is exactly an equality-form linear program. The matrix
\verb|self.A| stores the registered facts as columns. The vector
\verb|b_eq| stores the target equality we want to reproduce. Solving
\[
A_{\mathrm{eq}}x=b_{\mathrm{eq}}, \qquad x\geq0
\]
tells AR which facts were actually needed.

There is one technical wrinkle. In ordinary linear algebra one may combine
equations with positive or negative coefficients, but \verb|linprog| uses
non-negative variables. Therefore \verb|Table.register| stores two columns
per fact: one for using the fact forward and one for using it backward. The
signed coefficient is the difference between those two non-negative
weights.

\examplepart{Back to the bisector example}
For the worked example above, the registered facts give the following matrix:
\begin{lstlisting}
       fact1+  fact1-  fact2+  fact2-  fact3+  fact3-
d(ac)  [ 1      -1      -1       1      1      -1   ]
d(cd)  [-2       2       0       0      0       0   ]
d(bc)  [ 1      -1      -1       1     -2       2   ]
pi     [ 0       0       1      -1      1      -1   ]
d(ce)  [ 0       0       2      -2      0       0   ]
d(ab)  [ 0       0       0       0      1      -1   ]
\end{lstlisting}
Asking \emph{why} \(d(CD)-d(CE)=\tfrac12\pi\) produces a target vector with
\(+1\) on \verb|d(cd)|, \(-1\) on \verb|d(ce)|, and \(-\tfrac12\) on
\verb|pi|. Solving the equality-form LP gives:
\begin{lstlisting}
x* = (0, 0.5, 0, 0.5, 0, 0)      objective = -1
\end{lstlisting}
Thus \emph{fact1} and \emph{fact2} are used, each in the backward direction
with weight \(1/2\), while the distractor \emph{fact3} receives weight
\(0\). This is the AR analogue of the farmer's irrelevant mix \(R\): it is
available, but it is not needed to produce the target. The proof traceback
therefore reports exactly \texttt{['fact1', 'fact2']}.

\subsection{A Closer Look at the Language Model}\label{sec: lm.py}

DD and AR derive consequences over the points currently available, but they
cannot invent a new point. Part~I therefore gave the language model one narrow
job: rank candidate rabbits, after which DD+AR verifies the resulting state.
We now move from that classroom abstraction to the representations, weights,
and search interface used by the reference implementation and our
reconstruction.

The original paper \cite{trinh2024solving} reports generating about one
billion random diagrams and reducing them through proof search and
deduplication to roughly \(100\) million unique examples. About \(9\) million
used at least one auxiliary point according to the paper's
premise-versus-proof dependency test. Those examples should not be confused
with our stronger ``strict'' subset: our filter additionally requires the
visible problem to saturate unsolved and the restored problem to solve under
the same configured DD+AR budget.

\begin{lecturebox}{From the classroom pipeline to the reference system}
Part~I explained the mathematical map
\[
(\text{visible state},\text{goal})
\longmapsto
\text{auxiliary construction}.
\]
The remainder of Part~III supplies the details deliberately postponed there:
the compact token representation, exact generator functions,
dependency-aware hiding, strict filtering, corpus preparation, tokenizer,
model training, and beam-search inference.
\end{lecturebox}

\textbf{What we can verify directly.} We downloaded DeepMind's official
checkpoint and tokenizer with the official script, verified their hashes, and
ran them through a separate official JAX/Meliad checkout. We did \emph{not}
independently convert those weights. The official configuration is a
$12$-layer sequence model with hidden size $1024$, $8$ attention heads,
relative-position information, exactly $152{,}072{,}288$ parameters, and a
757-piece tokenizer. The legacy 608MB ChatLLM \verb|.bin| used by
AlphaGeometryRE is a distinct third-party conversion and should not be treated
as the provenance source for the official checkpoint.  In the original
AlphaGeometry loop and in our PyTorch adapter, the model input is the current
constructive problem statement, including any auxiliary constructions accepted
in earlier search rounds; it is not a serialization of DD+AR's entire derived
fact closure.  Both that input and the output (a proposed construction) use
the same compact token format seen throughout these notes,
e.g. \verb|e : C a c e 02 C b d e 03 ;| for
\verb|e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d|. The selected backend repeatedly
retains only a fixed number of the most likely partial strings---a procedure
called beam search---and returns a handful of scored candidates.
\Cref{sec: Running on Windows} showed this loop end to
end.

\textbf{What our reconstruction had to choose.} The public materials do not
fix every engineering decision needed to recreate a corpus. We therefore had
to choose how to sample constructions, how large each diagram should be,
which derived facts count as interesting goals, how aggressively to
deduplicate related examples, and how to format the final input-output
strings. The prototype section below records those choices rather than
presenting them as features of DeepMind's private pipeline.

\subsection{A Closer Look at \texttt{graph.py}}\label{sec: graph.py}

Every fact and table used so far has to live somewhere between calls to
\texttt{dd.py} and \texttt{ar.py}. That somewhere is a single \texttt{Graph}
object, built once per problem and threaded through the entire solve: it
holds every point, line, and segment seen so far, the
\texttt{atable}/\texttt{rtable}/\texttt{dtable} from \Cref{sec: ar.py}, and a
\texttt{cache} dictionary for quick dependency lookups. DD and AR only ever
read from and write into this one object.

\textbf{Two different kinds of ``related''.} The proof graph distinguishes
two relationships that are easy to conflate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Adjacency} (\texttt{edge\_graph}, queried via
          \texttt{neighbors()}): a genuine structural link, e.g. ``point $P$
          lies on line $\ell$.'' This is the actual geometric picture.
    \item \emph{Equivalence} (\texttt{merge\_graph}, queried via
          \texttt{rep()}): a record that two nodes have been \emph{proven
          equal} - two lines turning out to be the same line, or two lengths
          turning out to be the same length. This has nothing to do with
          adjacency.
\end{itemize}
Equivalence is maintained as a union-find structure, documented directly in
\texttt{geometry.py}'s \texttt{Node} class:
\begin{lstlisting}
  a -> b -
          \
       c -> d -> e -> f -> g

d.merged_to = e
d.rep = g
d.merged_from = {a, b, c, d}
d.equivs = {a, b, c, d, e, f, g}
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{rep()} just walks the chain of \texttt{rep\_by} pointers to the
representative; two nodes count as ``the same'' exactly when they share one.

\textbf{Where $d(\cdot)$ and $l(\cdot)$ actually come from.}
\Cref{sec: d(AB)} and \Cref{sec: ar.py} both used $d(AB)$ as notation for
line $AB$'s direction. It is not just notation - \texttt{connect\_val} builds
that exact string:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle]
def connect_val(self, node, deps):
    if node._val:
        return node._val
    name = None
    if isinstance(node, Line):
        name = 'd(' + node.name + ')'
    if isinstance(node, Angle):
        name = 'm(' + node.name + ')'
    if isinstance(node, Segment):
        name = 'l(' + node.name + ')'
    if isinstance(node, Ratio):
        name = 'r(' + node.name + ')'
    v = self.new_node(gm.val_type(node), name)
    self.connect(node, v, deps=deps)
    return v
\end{lstlisting}
Every \texttt{Line}, \texttt{Segment}, \texttt{Angle}, and \texttt{Ratio}
node has a separate \emph{value node} - its direction, length, measure, or
ratio - reachable as \texttt{node.\_val}. The geometric object and its value
are deliberately kept apart, because two different segments can share a
length without being the same segment.

\textbf{Why \texttt{cong} merges lengths, not segments.} This split is what
makes congruence work correctly. Proving $AB \cong CD$ calls:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pythonstyle]
def make_equal(self, x, y, deps):
    self.connect_val(x, deps=None)
    self.connect_val(y, deps=None)
    vx, vy = x._val, y._val
    self.merge([vx, vy], deps)
\end{lstlisting}
which merges \texttt{x.\_val} and \texttt{y.\_val} - the two \emph{Length}
nodes - not \texttt{x} and \texttt{y} themselves. Afterwards $A,B,C,D$ are
still four distinct points and $AB$, $CD$ are still two distinct segments,
but \texttt{rep()} on their Length nodes now agrees, under a name like
\texttt{l(ab)}. Checking ``are $AB$ and $CD$ congruent?'' later is then a
single \texttt{rep()} comparison, however many other segments have
separately been proven equal to either one.

\begin{conceptcheck}{reference-graph}
What is the difference between adjacency and equivalence in the reference
graph? If \verb|cong A B C D| is proved, why should it merge the length-value
nodes rather than the segment nodes?
\end{conceptcheck}

\section{Our AlphaGeometry-Style Prototype}\label{sec: Our Prototype}

Part~I developed the pipeline at classroom scale. We now revisit the same
ideas at the level of the actual repository, where every filtering decision,
serialization step, and training artifact can be inspected.

AlphaGeometryRE gives us a working symbolic solver and an interface to a
released language model. Our prototype adds the part that is most useful for
an undergraduate course: a complete, inspectable route from randomly
constructed diagrams to a language model that proposes rabbits. It is a
smaller reconstruction of the idea in AlphaGeometry, not an attempt to recover
DeepMind's private dataset exactly.

There are two reasons to build our own version even though a released model is
available. First, a model file does not explain where its examples came from.
Second, a course project should let us inspect every mathematical claim before
we ask a neural network to imitate it. In our system, DD+AR creates and checks
the proofs; the language model is trained only after those symbolic steps have
produced an auditable dataset.

\begin{lecturebox}{The classroom principle, now implemented}
We generate a problem \emph{backwards}. We first construct a larger diagram in
which DD+AR can prove a statement. We then hide a construction used by that
proof. The remaining visible problem becomes the input to the language model,
and the hidden construction becomes its desired output.
\end{lecturebox}

We will describe the prototype one file at a time. The first file is
\verb|src/generate_synthetic_data.py|. Later files make the checks stricter,
deduplicate the examples, translate them into the language-model vocabulary,
and train the model. The first subsection revisits the running orthocenter
example from Part~I at the level of concrete functions, stored fields, and
audit checks.

\subsection{The First Generator: \texttt{generate\_synthetic\_data.py}}
\label{sec: generate synthetic data.py}

The job of \verb|generate_synthetic_data.py| is to produce
\keyterm{auxiliary-construction candidates}. Each output row has the form
\[
  \text{visible geometry problem}
  \quad\longmapsto\quad
  \text{construction hidden from that problem}.
\]
The word ``candidate'' is important. At this stage we know that one traced
proof uses the hidden point. It is still possible that DD+AR could find a
different proof without it. We return to this distinction below.

\subsubsection{A map of the file}

The generator is long, but most of its logic is organized around the following
functions.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.34\textwidth}p{0.56\textwidth}}
\textbf{Function} & \textbf{Mathematical role} \\
\hline
\texttt{load\_defs\_rules} & Load the construction language and the DD rules
from \texttt{defs.txt} and \texttt{rules.txt}. \\
\texttt{sample\_random\_problem} & Build a valid random sequence of geometric
constructions. \\
\texttt{run\_ddar} & Run DD+AR on the full diagram and record whether the
closure saturated or stopped at a resource limit. \\
\texttt{enumerate\_candidate\_goals} & Select true facts in the completed
proof graph that can be turned into theorem statements. \\
\texttt{traceback\_goal} & Trace a selected fact backwards to the premises and
constructed points used by its proof. \\
\texttt{make\_example} & Hide proof-only points and serialize the visible
problem and target construction. \\
\texttt{iter\_examples} & Repeat the experiment with reproducible random seeds
and yield accepted JSON records one at a time. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Let us follow one pass through these functions.

\examplepart{Step 1: use the same mathematical language as the solver}

The generator does not invent a second collection of geometric operations. It
loads \verb|data/defs.txt| and \verb|data/rules.txt|, the same files used by
\verb|alphageometry.py|. Consequently, a construction sampled during data
generation has exactly the same meaning when it is later proposed by the
trained language model.

The conservative construction library contains familiar operations such as
\verb|midpoint|, \verb|foot|, \verb|on_line|, \verb|on_circle|,
\verb|intersection_ll|, \verb|incenter|, and \verb|orthocenter|. The
\verb|expanded| and \verb|all| settings allow more definitions from
\verb|defs.txt|. This choice lets a small classroom run begin with a controlled
vocabulary while a larger run can generate more varied diagrams.

\examplepart{Step 2: construct a random but valid diagram}

Ordinarily, \verb|sample_random_problem| starts with a triangle. It then picks
a construction name, chooses existing points as its inputs, and assigns the
next unused lowercase names to its outputs. For example, part of a sampled
sequence might be
\begin{lstlisting}
a b c = triangle a b c;
d = midpoint d a b;
e = foot e c a b;
f = on_circle f d e
\end{lstlisting}

Here ``random'' does not mean that we choose unrelated numerical coordinates.
That would almost never produce exact equal lengths, perpendicular lines, or
four concyclic points.  Instead, we randomly choose from constructions whose
geometric consequences are known.  For instance, a midpoint automatically
introduces a collinearity and two equal lengths, while a foot introduces a
collinearity and a perpendicularity.  Since every new construction uses
points already present in the diagram, these facts can overlap and DD+AR can
combine them.  The most productive sequences are those that eventually
``close a loop,'' for example by intersecting two lines developed in different
parts of the construction.

Random syntax is not enough. A proposed construction might ask for the
intersection of parallel lines, reuse points that are required to be distinct,
or place two numerical points almost on top of one another. Before keeping a
new clause, \verb|dependencies_hold| checks its formal hypotheses and
\verb|add_clause_safely| asks the numerical graph to realize it. Failed
attempts are discarded; they are an expected part of random generation, not a
mathematical theorem being declared false.

This incremental check is one of the most important implementation choices.
If we generated a long string first and checked it only at the end, one bad
construction would waste the entire diagram. Instead, the program keeps the
valid prefix and tries a different next step.

Validity still does not guarantee an interesting theorem.  A short random
sequence may contain only facts that follow immediately from its definitions,
whereas a very long sequence may be expensive and full of redundant facts.
Uniformly sampling construction names can also produce a skewed corpus,
because some constructions are much easier to realize than others.  Our
pipeline deals with this by generating many diagrams, balancing and capping
the kinds of conclusions mined from each one, and later keeping only the rows
that pass symbolic and numerical checks.  Thus the intelligence is not in a
single random draw; it is in constructing legally and filtering aggressively.

\examplepart{Step 3: ask DD+AR what follows}

Once the full construction sequence has been built, \verb|run_ddar| constructs
its \verb|Graph| and runs the same DD+AR loop used to solve olympiad problems.
There is no theorem goal yet. We are asking for the \emph{closure} of the
diagram: all facts that DD and AR can derive within the chosen limits.

The return value is a \verb|ClosureResult|. Besides the graph and its derived
dependencies, it records whether DD+AR saturated, hit a level limit, or timed
out. This makes resource failures visible in the data audit. In the later
production generator we reject non-saturated closures rather than silently
treating an interrupted search as complete.

\examplepart{Step 4: turn true facts into possible theorem goals}

The graph may now contain thousands of true facts. The function
\verb|enumerate_candidate_goals| extracts a manageable collection involving
predicates such as \verb|coll|, \verb|cyclic|, \verb|cong|, \verb|midp|,
\verb|para|, and \verb|perp|. It reads these facts from the graph's lines,
circles, length classes, direction classes, and DD+AR dependencies instead of
blindly trying every tuple of points.

For example, four points stored on the same circle give a possible
\verb|cyclic| goal, while two distinct lines in the same direction class give
a possible \verb|para| goal. The optional ``orphan point'' score favors goals
whose own points do not already depend on every point in the diagram. Such a
goal has room for a proof to pass through a point that is not visible in its
statement.

\examplepart{Step 5: trace one proof backwards}

For a selected goal $G$, \verb|traceback_goal| calls the existing proof
traceback machinery. The result separates
\begin{itemize}
    \item the setup facts needed to state and build the visible problem;
    \item auxiliary setup facts involving points outside that setup; and
    \item the sequence of deductions leading to $G$.
\end{itemize}
If a point occurs in the traced proof but not in the dependency closure of the
goal's setup points, \path{hidden_point_names} marks it as a possible rabbit.
This is dependency analysis, not a guess made from the numerical picture.

\examplepart{Step 6: hide the rabbit without leaving broken references}

Suppose point $e$ is selected as hidden and a later construction of $f$ uses
$e$. We cannot remove the clause defining $e$ while leaving the clause defining
$f$: the visible problem would refer to an unknown point. The function
\verb|split_visible_aux_and_dropped| therefore removes the hidden
construction and every later clause that depends on it. The construction that
introduces the proof-only point becomes the target; other clauses removed only
because they depend on it are recorded separately for auditing.

The goal itself is rejected if it mentions a removed point. The student model
must receive a well-formed problem whose conclusion can be read without
already knowing the answer construction.

\subsubsection{A small example}

Consider the full, solved construction
\begin{lstlisting}
a b c = triangle a b c;
d = on_tline d b a c, on_tline d c a b;
e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d
? perp a d b c
\end{lstlisting}
Here $D$ is defined using two altitudes. The goal asks for the missing altitude,
$AD\perp BC$. The proof traced by DD+AR passes through $E$, the intersection of
$AC$ and $BD$. Since $E$ does not occur in the theorem statement, the generator
can form the pair
\begin{lstlisting}
visible_problem:
a b c = triangle a b c;
d = on_tline d b a c, on_tline d c a b
? perp a d b c

target_auxiliary:
e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d
\end{lstlisting}
This is precisely the orthocenter rabbit seen earlier in the notes. During
training, the text before the arrow is the input and the construction of $E$
is the desired output. During proof search, the model may propose $E$, but
DD+AR must still rebuild the graph and prove $AD\perp BC$.

\subsubsection{What is stored in one output row?}

The output format is JSONL: each line is one independent JSON object. Important
fields include
\begin{itemize}
    \item \verb|visible_problem|: the problem shown to the future model;
    \item \verb|target_auxiliary|: the hidden construction the model should
          learn to propose;
    \item \verb|full_problem|: the original solved diagram, retained for audit;
    \item \verb|goal| and \verb|hidden_points|;
    \item \verb|proof_steps|: the symbolic proof found by traceback;
    \item \verb|removed_dependent_clauses|: constructions removed to keep the
          visible problem well formed; and
    \item \verb|seed| and diagnostic fields, which make failures reproducible.
\end{itemize}
The file is flushed regularly, so a long CPU run leaves usable intermediate
rows even if it is stopped before reaching its requested size. Different CPU
workers use different seeds and output files; their shards are merged later.
The geometry generation itself does not need a GPU.

\subsubsection{Candidate does not yet mean necessary}

There is a subtle logical issue. Traceback proves
\[
  \text{``DD+AR found a proof that uses }e\text{.''}
\]
It does \emph{not} prove
\[
  \text{``every DD+AR proof available to our solver needs }e\text{.''}
\]
The visible problem may have a second proof that the traceback did not select.
For a small experiment, the flag \verb|--require_unsolved_without_aux| makes
\verb|generate_synthetic_data.py| rebuild and solve the visible problem before
accepting a row. That extra DD+AR run is expensive at scale. Our production
pipeline therefore first generates candidates quickly and then passes them to
\verb|filter_strict_auxiliary.py|, which performs the stronger two-sided test:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item DD+AR must fail on the visible problem without the rabbit;
    \item after restoring the rabbit, DD+AR must solve the goal.
\end{enumerate}
Only rows passing both checks enter our strict auxiliary fine-tuning set. This
distinction explains why the number of genuine training examples is much
smaller than the number of initially plausible candidates.

\subsubsection{Running a one-example teaching experiment}

The file contains a known-good orthocenter construction as a smoke seed. A
small auditable run is
\begin{lstlisting}
python src/generate_synthetic_data.py \
  --num_examples 1 \
  --seed 1 \
  --max_attempts 20 \
  --curated_rate 1.0 \
  --max_level 1000 \
  --require_unsolved_without_aux \
  --diagnostics \
  --out outputs/synthetic_data/teaching_aux.jsonl
\end{lstlisting}
The JSONL file contains the accepted row. The neighboring diagnostics file
counts sampled diagrams, candidate goals, failed tracebacks, duplicate rows,
and each rejection reason. For a student, those rejection counts are as
important as the successful example: they show which mathematical conditions
the program is enforcing.

\subsubsection{Why are there three generator files?}

At this point, the repository contains three similarly named files:
\verb|generate_synthetic_data.py|, \verb|generate_pretraining_data.py|, and
\verb|generate_geometry_corpus.py|. They are not three competing
implementations of the same task. They reflect two different learning
objectives and a later efficiency improvement.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.30\textwidth}p{0.31\textwidth}p{0.29\textwidth}}
\textbf{File} & \textbf{Question answered} & \textbf{Output} \\
\hline
\texttt{generate\_synthetic\_data.py} & Which construction can be hidden from
a solved problem? & Visible problem $\mapsto$ candidate rabbit. \\
\texttt{generate\_pretraining\_data.py} & Which theorems and proofs can be
mined from a random diagram? & Constructed theorem $\mapsto$ symbolic proof. \\
\texttt{generate\_geometry\_corpus.py} & How can we produce both datasets
without solving every diagram twice? & Both streams from one shared DD+AR
closure. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{The auxiliary-construction stream.}
The file \verb|generate_synthetic_data.py| was our first generator. Its target
is a construction. It removes a proof-only point from a solved diagram and
creates an example of the form
\[
  \text{stuck visible problem}
  \quad\longmapsto\quad
  \text{useful auxiliary construction}.
\]
These rows are intended for the second training stage, when the model learns to
propose rabbits. The file also contains the common machinery for sampling
constructions, running DD+AR, enumerating goals, tracing proofs, and detecting
hidden points. The other generators import and reuse this machinery.

\paragraph{The general-proof pretraining stream.}
Before learning which rabbit to propose, the model benefits from learning the
formal language of geometry and the patterns that occur in symbolic proofs.
The file \path{generate_pretraining_data.py} creates this broader corpus. It
does not hide an auxiliary construction. Instead, it selects an ordinary true
conclusion from a DD+AR closure, traces its proof, and serializes an example of
the form
\[
  \text{constructed diagram and theorem}
  \quad\longmapsto\quad
  \text{symbolic proof}.
\]
Because no construction has to be inverted into a rabbit, this stream can use
more kinds of conclusions, including equal angles, equal ratios, similar
triangles, and congruent triangles. These examples teach syntax and geometric
proof patterns; they do not yet teach what to construct when the solver is
stuck.

\paragraph{The shared production generator.}
Running the two earlier files independently repeats the most expensive
operation: constructing a graph and saturating it with DD+AR. Our production
entry point, \path{generate_geometry_corpus.py}, builds each random diagram
once and then views the resulting closure in two ways:
\[
\begin{array}{c}
\text{one random construction sequence}\\
\downarrow\\
\text{one saturated DD+AR closure}
\end{array}
\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad
\begin{cases}
\text{general theorem/proof rows},\\
\text{auxiliary-construction candidates}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus, one expensive symbolic computation supplies many examples for both
training stages. The shared generator imports the construction and traceback
logic from \path{generate_synthetic_data.py} and the proof-row logic from
\path{generate_pretraining_data.py}.

The production file also applies safeguards that are especially important for
long runs. It rejects closures that did not saturate, numerically checks the
selected conclusions, limits how many examples one diagram or predicate can
contribute, and records audit counts. These controls prevent one unusually
dense diagram or one easy predicate from dominating the dataset.

\begin{lecturebox}{Which file should a student run?}
Use \path{generate_synthetic_data.py} to study one auxiliary example and
the hiding procedure. Use \path{generate_pretraining_data.py} for an
experiment concerned only with ordinary theorem/proof pretraining. For the
full reconstruction pipeline, use \path{generate_geometry_corpus.py}; it
produces both streams efficiently. Its auxiliary output is still passed to
\path{filter_strict_auxiliary.py} before fine-tuning.
\end{lecturebox}

\begin{lecturebox}{What this file contributes}
\path{generate_synthetic_data.py} is the bridge from symbolic geometry to
machine-learning data. It does not ask a language model to discover theorems.
It asks DD+AR to prove facts in random diagrams, uses proof dependencies to
identify a possible rabbit, and only then creates an input-output pair for a
future model.
\end{lecturebox}

\subsection{Strict Filtering and Shard Merging}
\label{sec:strict-filtering}

The production generator deliberately favors speed: traceback supplies
\emph{candidate} rabbits, and a separate program performs the expensive test
of whether they are genuinely useful.  This separation also lets many CPU
workers generate and filter independent files without sharing a large data
structure.

\subsubsection{Strict auxiliary filtering: is the rabbit needed?}

For every candidate row, \path{filter_strict_auxiliary.py} rebuilds the visible
problem and performs two experiments:
\[
\begin{array}{rcl}
\text{visible problem without rabbit} & \xrightarrow{\mathrm{DD+AR}} &
\text{must remain unsolved},\\
\text{visible problem with rabbit restored} & \xrightarrow{\mathrm{DD+AR}} &
\text{must be solved}.
\end{array}
\]
If DD+AR already proves the visible problem, the row is rejected: the hidden
point occurred in one traced proof but was not needed by the solver.  If DD+AR
still cannot prove the result after the point is restored, the row is also
rejected.  Timeouts, level limits, and construction errors are recorded
separately and are not accepted as evidence that a rabbit is necessary.

When a target contains several construction clauses, the filter can also find
a smaller sufficient subset.  It checks all subsets for small targets and
uses a greedy procedure for larger ones.  Finally, it traces the restored proof
again and stores the strict proof and solve diagnostics in the output row.

The word ``necessary'' here is relative to our formal system.  A retained row
shows that the configured DD+AR solver, using the rules and resource limits in
this repository, cannot solve the visible problem but can solve it after the
construction is restored.  It does not claim that no human proof, or no proof
in a stronger formal system, could avoid that point.  Repeating the numerical
rebuild with different seeds makes this test more conservative when a diagram
is sensitive to its numerical realization.

\subsubsection{\texttt{merge\_synthetic\_shards.py}: combine and audit}

A long run is split into many \keyterm{shards}: separate JSONL files written by
independent CPU workers.  In principle these files could simply be
concatenated, so shard merging is not a new mathematical stage.  Our merge
program nevertheless does three useful jobs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item it combines the selected shard files into one corpus;
    \item it removes duplicates, including copies that differ only by a
          consistent renaming of points; and
    \item it reports distributions of goal predicates, auxiliary construction
          types, numbers of target clauses, and auxiliary-point centrality.
\end{enumerate}
Here a \keyterm{canonical key} is a standard representation used to recognize
the same example under superficial changes. Points are renamed by a fixed
first-introduction convention, and each predicate is normalized using only its
valid mathematical symmetries. For example, every permutation of
\verb|coll A B C| represents the same collinearity fact. Construction clauses
are not sorted arbitrarily, because their order records which points exist
when a later construction is executed. Rows with the same canonical key can
therefore be deduplicated even when their original point names differ.

It can optionally discard rows in which the hidden point touches only a very
small fraction of the traced proof.  That is a quality heuristic, not a proof
of necessity; the strict solver test remains the mathematical filter.

Thus \verb|merge_synthetic_shards.py| is optional for a one-worker classroom
experiment, but important for a large distributed corpus.  In our production
pipeline we merge the \emph{strict} outputs, rather than treating the merge as
a substitute for strict filtering:
\[
\text{candidate shard}
\longrightarrow \text{strict filter}
\longrightarrow \text{strict shard}
\longrightarrow \text{merge and deduplicate}
\longrightarrow \text{LM training data}.
\]

\begin{conceptcheck}{strict-filter-and-merge}
What two solver outcomes must a row satisfy to pass strict filtering? Why is a
timeout not evidence that the rabbit is needed? What does shard merging add,
and why can it not replace the strict filter?
\end{conceptcheck}

\subsection{From Strict Geometry Rows to LM Text}
\label{sec:lm-data-pipeline}

The strict-filter output is mathematically suitable for training, but it is
not yet written in the representation expected by the AlphaGeometry language
model.  A row still uses the constructive language of \verb|defs.txt|, for
example
\begin{lstlisting}
e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d
\end{lstlisting}
The file \verb|synthetic_data_to_lm.py| is the representation bridge.  It does
not discover a new theorem or run DD+AR.  Instead, it expands each construction
into its basic constraints using \verb|defs.txt| and rewrites the result in the
compact AlphaGeometry token language consumed by the solver's inference
adapters. For example,
\verb|on_line e a c| contributes the fact that $A,C,E$ are collinear, written
\verb|C a c e|.  Similarly, \verb|on_tline d b a c| contributes a
perpendicularity, written with the token \verb|T|.

The converted JSON row contains three main strings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \verb|prompt|: the visible construction facts, the theorem goal, and
          the fixed marker \verb|{F1} x00| requesting an auxiliary prediction;
    \item \verb|target|: the hidden point expressed through the constraints
          that define it; and
    \item \verb|text|: \verb|prompt| and \verb|target| concatenated into the
          autoregressive training sequence.
\end{itemize}
The original constructive fields are retained beside these strings so that a
training example can still be audited.  The symbolic proof is not placed in
this auxiliary-prediction prompt: the model's task is only to propose a point,
and DD+AR remains responsible for checking whether that proposal proves the
goal.

\subsubsection{One concrete conversion}

Consider the strict example used by the repository's conversion test:
\begin{lstlisting}
visible_problem:
a b c = triangle a b c;
d = on_tline d b a c, on_tline d c a b
? perp a d b c

target_auxiliary:
e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d
\end{lstlisting}
The visible construction says that $BD\perp AC$ and $CD\perp AB$, while the
goal is $AD\perp BC$.  The target introduces $E$ as the intersection of $AC$
and $BD$.  The converter produces the following prompt (shown on several lines
only for readability):
\begin{lstlisting}
{S} a : ; b : ; c : ;
d : T a b c d 00 T a c b d 01
? T a d b c {F1} x00
\end{lstlisting}
Here \verb|{S}| begins the formal statement, \verb|T| means perpendicular, and
\verb|00| and \verb|01| are successive reference labels for the two visible
constraints.  The free triangle points have no additional basic constraints,
which explains the empty segments \verb|a :|, \verb|b :|, and \verb|c :|.
The target becomes
\begin{lstlisting}
e : C a c e 02 C b d e 03 ;
\end{lstlisting}
The token \verb|C| means collinear.  Thus the model is taught to answer:
``introduce a point $E$ with $A,C,E$ collinear and $B,D,E$ collinear.''  The
reference labels continue with \verb|02| and \verb|03| so that every constraint
in the combined sequence has its own number.  At inference time,
\verb|alphageometry.py| translates this constrained output back into the
constructive clause
\begin{lstlisting}
e = on_line e a c, on_line e b d
\end{lstlisting}
and rebuilds the graph before running DD+AR again.

By default the converter favors simple labels: it rejects targets containing
more than one point group and targets with more than two defining predicates.
The production command can explicitly allow multi-point or more complicated
targets.  These limits change the difficulty and diversity of the learning
problem; they do not change whether the underlying row passed the strict
geometric test.

\subsection{Preparing the Train, Validation, and Test Sets}

After conversion, we have a collection of independent JSON rows containing
\verb|prompt|, \verb|target|, and \verb|text|.  The file
\verb|prepare_lm_dataset.py| performs collection-level housekeeping.  It does
not alter the geometry, tokenize the strings, or train a model.  Its tasks are
to remove repeated training strings and divide the remaining examples into
reproducible train, validation, and test sets.

The split assignment for a retained key is computed by hashing a chosen field
together with a random seed, so rerunning with the same retained rows and seed
gives the same partition. There is one caveat: when the deduplication key and
split key differ, input order may determine which representative of a duplicate
group survives. Canonicalize and globally deduplicate first when strict
order-independence is required.
The training set is used to update model parameters, the validation set to
monitor and select a model during development, and the test set is held back
for the final evaluation.

The choice \verb|--split_key diagram_id| is particularly important.  One
random construction can yield many closely related theorem and auxiliary
rows.  If those rows were split independently, one version of a diagram might
enter the training set while an almost identical version entered the test
set.  This \keyterm{data leakage} would make the measured test performance
misleadingly high.  Splitting by \verb|diagram_id| keeps every row mined from
the same original diagram in the same partition.

For each partition the program writes two parallel files:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \verb|train.jsonl|, \verb|val.jsonl|, and \verb|test.jsonl| retain
          identifiers and audit metadata; and
    \item \verb|train.txt|, \verb|val.txt|, and \verb|test.txt| contain one
          complete LM training sequence per line.
\end{itemize}
A neighboring \verb|summary.json| records how many rows were read, removed as
duplicates, and assigned to each partition.  The plain-text files are the
inputs to tokenizer and model training, while the JSONL versions let us trace
a line back to the geometry that produced it.

\subsection{Training the Geometry Tokenizer}

The files produced so far still contain text.  A transformer receives integer
IDs, so \verb|train_geometry_tokenizer.py| learns a fixed dictionary
\[
\text{geometry text}\longrightarrow\text{tokens}\longrightarrow
\text{integer IDs}.
\]
It combines the supplied pretraining and auxiliary text files and uses
\keyterm{SentencePiece}, a software library for constructing token
dictionaries.  We select its \verb|word| mode, which treats
whitespace-separated pieces as candidate tokens.  This is well suited to our
DSL because its symbols are already separated by spaces: we want \verb|T|,
\verb|C|, \verb|a|, \verb|02|, and \verb|;| to be stable units rather than
fragments of ordinary-language words.

For example, consider again the auxiliary target
\begin{lstlisting}
e : C a c e 02 C b d e 03 ;
\end{lstlisting}
Our selected tokenizer converts it to the following pieces, where
\verb|<space>| denotes SentencePiece's special leading-space marker:
\begin{lstlisting}
<space>e  <space>:  <space>C  <space>a  <space>c
<space>e  <space>02 <space>C  <space>b  <space>d
<space>e  <space>03 <space>;
\end{lstlisting}
The corresponding integer IDs in our current model are
\begin{lstlisting}
271 145 149 266 268 271 168 149 267 270 271 169 144
\end{lstlisting}
The transformer receives this integer sequence.  It later learns vector
embeddings and geometric patterns for these IDs; the tokenizer itself does not
learn what collinearity means.

Some syntax is too important to leave to frequency-based vocabulary learning.
The script therefore registers tokens such as \verb|{S}|, \verb|{F1}|,
\verb|{QED}|, \verb|x00|, the predicate symbols, punctuation, and the reference
labels \verb|00| through \verb|99| as user-defined symbols.  It also assigns
four conventional special pieces
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|p{0.47\textwidth}}
\textbf{Piece} & \textbf{ID} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\texttt{<unk>} & 0 & ``unknown'': used when input cannot be represented by the
vocabulary. \\
\texttt{<s>} & 1 & ``start of sequence'': placed before the first ordinary
token.  The code calls its ID \texttt{bos\_id}, for ``beginning of sequence.'' \\
\texttt{</s>} & 2 & ``end of sequence'': placed after the last ordinary token.
The code calls its ID \texttt{eos\_id}. \\
\texttt{<pad>} & 3 & ``padding'': a meaningless filler used to make shorter
sequences in a batch have the same length.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This guarantees that required AlphaGeometry syntax is not accidentally split
or replaced by the unknown token.

Training writes two files.  The binary \verb|.model| file contains the
tokenizer used by the model code.  The human-readable \verb|.vocab| file has
one piece per line.  Its first column is the piece and its second column is a
SentencePiece score, roughly a log-probability used when selecting a
segmentation.  The score is not the token ID and is not a raw corpus count;
the zero-based line position gives the ID.  Required user-defined symbols
normally have score zero.

Although our selected artifact is named
\verb|ag_word_757_pretrain_rich_v2|, it contains 368 pieces.  The command
requested a vocabulary size of 757, but the default does not impose a hard
limit, so SentencePiece retained only the useful and required pieces available
in this corpus.  Finally, the script encodes several representative geometry
strings and fails if a required symbol is missing or becomes \verb|<unk>|.
This self-check prevents an incompatible tokenizer from reaching the much more
expensive language-model training stage.

\subsection{Model Training}
\label{sec:model-training}

The file \verb|train_demo_lm.py| trains our PyTorch language model.  Despite
the historical word ``demo'' in its name, the same configurable trainer was
used for the selected educational reconstruction.  PyTorch is the
neural-network software library used to represent the model, differentiate its
calculations, and update its parameters.  A \keyterm{parameter} is simply a
number inside the network that is adjusted during training.

The model is a \keyterm{decoder-only causal transformer}.  A transformer is a
neural network for sequences: it repeatedly lets the representation of each
token gather information from other positions.  ``Causal'' means that a
position may use only the tokens to its left, never the future answer.
``Decoder-only'' means that there is one such network whose task is to
continue a prefix, rather than a separate network for reading an input and a
second network for writing an output.  For every possible next token, the
model produces a probability; all of these probabilities sum to one.

For each line of the training file, the data loader uses the SentencePiece
model to create
\[
  \underbrace{\texttt{<s>}}_{\text{start of sequence}},
  t_1,t_2,\ldots,t_n,
  \underbrace{\texttt{</s>}}_{\text{end of sequence}}.
\]
Here $t_i$ is the integer ID of the $i$th ordinary token.  The start and end
symbols are the \verb|<s>| and \verb|</s>| pieces defined above.  At every
position, the \keyterm{label} is the correct next token.  The code obtains the
labels by shifting the sequence one place to the left.  Thus, after seeing a
prefix such as \verb|e : C|, the correct label is \verb|a|, and the model is
rewarded for assigning \verb|a| high probability.

The \keyterm{context length} is the maximum number of tokens that the model can
read at once.  Longer lines are cut off; shorter ones receive the meaningless
\verb|<pad>| filler so that several examples can be placed in one rectangular
array, called a \keyterm{batch}.  Padding positions are ignored when measuring
the error.

Each token ID is first replaced by a learned list of numbers called a
\keyterm{token embedding}; a second learned list records the token's position
in the sequence.  In \keyterm{self-attention}, each position assigns weights
to earlier positions and forms a weighted combination of their information.
Several \keyterm{attention heads} do this in parallel, so different heads can
notice different relationships.  A causal mask sets every forbidden
future-looking weight to zero.  Each layer also contains a small
\keyterm{feed-forward network}, which transforms each position separately.
After several layers, an output calculation assigns one score to every token
in the vocabulary and converts the scores into probabilities.

The training \keyterm{loss} is a single number measuring prediction error;
lower is better.  We use next-token \keyterm{cross-entropy}, which imposes a
large penalty when the model assigns low probability to the correct next
token.  A \keyterm{gradient} tells us how each parameter should change to
decrease this loss.  \keyterm{AdamW} is the update algorithm, or
\keyterm{optimizer}, used to turn those gradients into parameter changes.
Adam is short for ``adaptive moment estimation'': it keeps running averages of
recent gradients and their squared sizes to choose a separate update scale for
each parameter.  AdamW combines this with decoupled \keyterm{weight decay}, a
gentle pressure toward smaller parameter values that can reduce memorization.

The \keyterm{learning rate} controls the overall size of an optimizer update.
During \keyterm{warmup} it rises gradually from a small value; afterwards our
\keyterm{cosine schedule} lowers it smoothly toward zero.  \keyterm{Gradient
accumulation} adds information from several small batches before making one
update, which imitates a larger batch when GPU memory is limited.
\keyterm{Gradient clipping} caps an unusually large update, and
\keyterm{mixed-precision training} performs many calculations with 16-bit
rather than 32-bit numbers to reduce GPU memory and often increase speed.
These are stability and efficiency devices; none changes the geometry
language being learned.  A GPU, or graphics processing unit, is a processor
designed to perform many numerical operations in parallel.  Training can also
run on an ordinary CPU, or central processing unit, but a large model is much
slower there.

The trainer records \verb|config.json| and \verb|metrics.jsonl| and regularly
saves a \keyterm{checkpoint}: a file containing the model parameters, the
optimizer's running averages, the step number, and the architecture.  Saving
the optimizer information allows a stopped run to resume with the same update
history.  Alternatively, we can load only the model parameters and begin a new
optimization stage.

\subsubsection{Our two training stages}

Our selected reconstruction follows the broad curriculum used by
AlphaGeometry:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pretraining.} The complete prepared corpus contains
          5,400,640 unique theorem/proof strings: 5,292,720 train, 54,144
          validation, and 53,776 test rows. Model updates use only the training
          partition. This stage teaches the formal vocabulary and recurring
          patterns of symbolic geometry.
    \item \textbf{Auxiliary fine-tuning.} The pretrained weights are then
          continued on 14,284 training examples from the strict auxiliary
          split, with 790 validation and 677 test examples (15,751 total).
          This specializes the model to continue a stuck problem with a useful
          construction.
\end{enumerate}
The selected run used 30,000 pretraining steps followed by 2,000 fine-tuning
steps.  Fine-tuning loaded the pretrained weights but reset the optimizer and
used a smaller learning rate.  Several fine-tuning settings were compared,
and the checkpoint was selected using \keyterm{validation loss}: the same
prediction-error measure computed on examples that were not used to update the
parameters. The intended evaluation protocol is to leave the test partition
and geometry benchmarks untouched until after this choice; retained run logs
should be used to audit whether an experiment followed that protocol.

\begin{lecturebox}{What is available in the separate course-code repository}
The separate course-code repository contains the complete 15,751-row
auxiliary split, but only a
deterministic 2,000-row sample of the pretraining corpus.  The full prepared
pretraining text (about 5.35 GB in total) and the optimizer-inclusive
checkpoints (about 1.82 GB each) are intentionally external release artifacts,
hash-pinned in \path{release/manifest-v4.json}.  Thus the Git checkout by
itself documents and exercises the pipeline, but it cannot reproduce the
selected full training run until the versioned release bundle and its
permanent download URL are published.
\end{lecturebox}

\end{conceptcheck}

\subsection{Inference: Connecting the Model Back to DD+AR}
\label{sec:hybrid-inference}

This is the concrete repository implementation of the symbolic validation
loop in \Cref{sec: Integrating the language model}. Part~I stated the
invariant---a proposal is not a proof---whereas this subsection identifies the
exact adapters, token representation, beam widths, and search limits that
enforce it.

Three files have distinct roles at inference time:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \verb|generate_demo_lm.py| is a standalone diagnostic sampler.  It
          loads a checkpoint and generates text, but it does not attempt a
          geometry proof.  Its \keyterm{temperature} setting controls
          randomness: smaller values concentrate probability on the most
          likely token, while larger values produce more varied output.
          \keyterm{Top-$k$ sampling} permits a random choice only among the $k$
          highest-probability next tokens.
    \item \verb|pytorch_lm_inference.py| is the adapter used by our solver.  It
          loads the checkpoint and tokenizer.  An \keyterm{adapter} is a small
          piece of code that presents our PyTorch model through the input and
          output interface expected by \verb|alphageometry.py|.
    \item \verb|alphageometry.py| owns the actual hybrid proof search.  It
          translates model strings into constructions, rebuilds the geometry
          graph, and asks DD+AR to verify them.
\end{itemize}
The older \verb|lm_inference.py| provides the analogous adapter for the legacy
third-party AlphaGeometryRE conversion that runs in ChatLLM, a separate
lightweight inference program. It is not the official checkpoint path and is
not the backend trained by our PyTorch pipeline.

The solver uses \keyterm{beam search} rather than drawing one random
continuation, at two different levels.  Inside the PyTorch decoder,
\verb|--batch_size| controls how many partial token strings survive each
generation step.  Outside the decoder, \verb|--beam_size| controls how many
constructed geometry states survive after candidates are parsed and tested.
The first is the \emph{token beam}; the second is the \emph{proof-state beam}.
Together they let the solver try several promising rabbits without
enumerating every possible string or construction sequence.

For one problem, the hybrid inference loop is
\begin{enumerate}
    \item run DD+AR on the visible problem and stop immediately if it succeeds;
    \item if it is stuck, append \verb|{F1} x00| to the compact problem prompt;
    \item use beam search to generate several candidate strings, stopping each
          candidate at \verb|;| or the decode limit;
    \item translate a constrained output such as
          \verb|e : C a c e 02 C b d e 03 ;| back into a constructive clause;
    \item reject malformed or unrealizable constructions, rebuild the graph
          for each valid candidate, and run DD+AR again; and
    \item if necessary, retain the highest-scoring states and repeat up to the
          requested construction depth.
\end{enumerate}
The LM therefore never certifies a theorem.  It ranks plausible rabbits; the
symbolic system supplies the proof certificate.  Both beam widths, construction
depth, context length, and \keyterm{decode length}---the maximum number of
tokens permitted in one generated construction---affect the solve rate as well
as the runtime.  Together these limits form the \keyterm{search budget}, so a
benchmark result must report them.

\subsection{Why a 152M-Parameter Model?}

The public AlphaGeometry configuration uses 12 repeated transformer layers.
Its \keyterm{hidden dimension} is 1024, meaning that each token is represented
inside the network by 1024 numbers.  It has 8 attention heads---eight parallel
ways of combining earlier-token information---and a feed-forward dimension of
4096, the width of the per-position network inside each layer.  These choices
give approximately 152 million trainable parameters.  We chose the same
headline dimensions for our selected reconstruction so that model capacity,
the amount of adjustable numerical machinery available to learn patterns,
would be roughly comparable.  Our selected model has exactly 152,057,856
parameters.

Matching the parameter count does \emph{not} reproduce DeepMind's model.  The
original used Meliad, DeepMind's software framework for this model, and
\keyterm{relative-position attention}, in which attention receives information
about the distance between two tokens.  It also used a 1024-token context and
vastly more training data, with ReLU activations and 5\% dropout. Our PyTorch
model instead uses learned absolute position vectors, GELU activations, 10\%
dropout in the selected configuration, and a 512-token context. The shared
size is a useful comparison point, not a claim
of architectural or performance equivalence.

There is no mathematical requirement that an auxiliary-point model have 152
million parameters.  The trainer exposes \verb|d_model|, \verb|n_layers|,
\verb|n_heads|, and \verb|d_ff| precisely so that different sizes can be used.
For our 368-piece vocabulary, some concrete choices are:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|c|c|c|c|r}
\textbf{Use} & \textbf{Layers} & \textbf{Hidden} & \textbf{Feed-forward} &
\textbf{Context} & \textbf{Parameters} \\
\hline
small teaching run & 4 & 256 & 1024 & 192 & 3,302,912 \\
trainer default & 6 & 384 & 1536 & 256 & 10,887,168 \\
medium experiment & 8 & 512 & 2048 & 512 & 25,670,656 \\
selected reconstruction & 12 & 1024 & 4096 & 512 & 152,057,856
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A smaller model trains faster, uses less GPU memory and disk space, and is much
easier for students to reproduce.  It may be worse at \keyterm{generalizing},
meaning applying learned patterns to new diagrams rather than merely repeating
training examples, or at keeping several useful construction patterns alive in
beam search.  A larger model is more expensive and, with only 15,751 strict
auxiliary examples, can \keyterm{overfit}: it can memorize the training set
while failing to improve on unseen problems.

The principled way to choose the size is an \keyterm{ablation experiment}: run
several otherwise similar trainings while deliberately changing the model
size.  The comparison should use similar data and \keyterm{token budgets},
meaning similar total numbers of training tokens processed.  We then compare
not only validation loss but also the rate of syntactically valid constructions
and the number of previously DD+AR-unsolved problems completed by the full
hybrid system.  For a course project, a 10--30M parameter model is a reasonable
reproducible choice; the 152M model is useful when the goal is to mimic
AlphaGeometry's published scale more closely.

\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:architecture}: Who owns each task?}

In the course solver, ``line $AB$'' is shorthand for using the point pair
$(A,B)$; it is not a stored geometric object. The numerical construction code
rejects an intersection of parallel lines. The proof graph remembers why a
particular equality of two segment lengths has been proved. AR combines
equal-angle equations. Construction search or the language model proposes a
new midpoint, after which DD+AR must verify its consequences.

\hypertarget{cca:angle-convention}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:angle-convention}: Ordinary and directed angles}

The rays $BA$ and $BA'$ point in opposite directions, so
\[
\angle_0ABC+\angle_0A'BC=\pi.
\]
Thus replacing a ray endpoint by a point on the opposite ray changes an
ordinary angle to its supplement.  The lines $AB$ and $A'B$ are nevertheless
the same unoriented line: any real direction representatives for them differ
by $\pi$, so they define the same element of
$\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z$.  Parallelism is therefore the linear relation
\[
d(AB)-d(CD)=0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z.
\]

\hyperref[sec: Angle Conventions]{Back to Angle Conventions}.

\hypertarget{cca:objects}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:objects}: The points-only representation}

The solver adds a certified congruence predicate relating $A,B,C,D$; it does
not create or merge segment objects, and all four named points remain the
points they were. What has been proved is the equality of two numerical
length values, $|AB|=|CD|$. With $n$ points there are $O(n^2)$ point pairs and
therefore $O(n^4)$ ordered pairs of point pairs. Explicitly materializing every
possible ratio would consequently require quartic storage even before most of
those ratios are used.

\hypertarget{cca:predicates}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:predicates}: Predicate symmetries and roles}

\verb|coll A B C| is invariant under every permutation of its three points,
so sorting them preserves its meaning. The ordered positions in
\verb|contri A B C P Q R| record the vertex correspondences
$A\leftrightarrow P$, $B\leftrightarrow Q$, and $C\leftrightarrow R$; blindly
sorting those arguments can destroy the claim. The relation
\verb|perp A B C D| is a symbolic geometric fact, \verb|ncoll A B C| is a
symbolic non-degeneracy guard, and coordinate closeness is only a numerical
diagnostic.

\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:problem-data}: The course problem state}

The state contains the point dictionary, canonical predicate facts,
justifications, one goal, and optional numerical drawing data. A construction
may depend only on points already in that dictionary; a future point is
undefined and would break reproducible construction order. Only the symbolic
dependency graph and justified AR rows may certify the theorem. Coordinates
can draw or validate one realization, but they are not a proof.

\hypertarget{cca:dd}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:dd}: DD saturation}

A rule variable is a placeholder such as $A$ in a theorem pattern; matching
assigns it to a concrete named point in the current problem. Saturation means
that no configured DD rule can add another fact over the points currently
available. It does \emph{not} mean that the goal is false or mathematically
impossible, nor that a stronger rule library, AR, or a useful auxiliary point
could not prove it.

\hypertarget{cca:conjectures}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:conjectures}: Numerical candidates}

Rounding, a tolerance that is too generous, a nearly degenerate diagram, or an
accidental special realization can make two lengths look equal even when the
equality does not follow from the assumptions. A fact selected from the
completed DD+AR closure already has a symbolic derivation. A coordinate bucket
provides only a promising conjecture that still needs such a derivation.

\hypertarget{cca:ar}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:ar}: Linearized geometry}

The equal-angle predicate becomes
\[
d(AB)-d(CD)-d(EF)+d(GH)=0
\qquad\text{in }\mathbb R/\pi\mathbb Z.
\]
For nonzero segment lengths, logarithms turn multiplication and division into
addition and subtraction. Thus an equality of ratios becomes a linear
equation
\[
\ell(AB)-\ell(CD)-\ell(EF)+\ell(GH)=0.
\]
``Full rank'' means the stored rows are linearly independent, not that the
table is square. Row reduction may discard derivation history; stored
dependencies recover it for proof traceback and checking. If the augmented
constant is $q\pi$, then $q$ is normalized modulo $1$: $q=0$ and $q=1$
differ by one whole $\pi$ and describe the same unoriented line-angle relation,
whereas $q=\tfrac12$ represents a perpendicular relation and is not zero.

\hypertarget{cca:basic-solver}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:basic-solver}: Sharing consequences}

An AR consequence can satisfy a premise of a DD rule, and the conclusion of
that DD rule can supply a new equation to AR. Running the engines once in two
isolated phases can therefore miss an alternating chain of consequences. Of
the four outcomes listed, only \emph{proved} supplies a positive theorem label.
Saturation, a resource limit, and an invalid realization are not proofs.

\hypertarget{cca:area-extension}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:area-extension}: An optional area engine}

The two course AR matrices handle linear relations among directions and
log-lengths. Signed areas and general polynomial identities require a
different representation, so area is not simply a third copy of the same
matrix. A sound extension needs, among other things, canonical area
expressions, inference or elimination rules, degeneracy checks, and dependency
tracking that can reconstruct a proof.

\hypertarget{cca:auxiliary}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:auxiliary}: Validating a rabbit}

Numerically, the midpoint is computed as
$M=(A+B)/2$ in coordinates. Symbolically, the construction contributes
\verb|coll M A B| and \verb|cong M A M B|. Requiring two circles to intersect
is essential because otherwise the requested point does not exist; tangent
and two-intersection cases also affect branch selection. After a model proposes
a rabbit, the system must parse and validate it, realize it stably, rebuild or
extend the symbolic state, and rerun DD+AR. The proposal is accepted as a
solution only when that symbolic run certifies the goal.

\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:data-cleaning}: Auditing classroom data}

\hypertarget{cca:formalize-menelaus}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:formalize-menelaus}: Formalizing Menelaus}

The incidence skeleton is
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: coll B D C; coll C E A; coll A F B;
Goal: coll D E F
\end{lstlisting}
For the converse, however, the setup must also assert
\[
 \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}
 \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}
 \frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}=-1.
\]
The course ratio table uses logarithms of positive lengths. It can encode the
magnitude of this product but loses the sign determined by point order on each
line. A sound full formalization therefore needs directed-ratio or betweenness
information, or a dedicated checked Menelaus predicate and rule.

\hypertarget{cca:formalize-pappus}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:formalize-pappus}: Formalizing Pappus}

One course-language formalization of the finite incidence pattern is
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: coll A E C; coll D B F;
        coll A B X; coll D E X;
        coll C D Y; coll F A Y;
        coll B C Z; coll E F Z;
Goal: coll X Y Z
\end{lstlisting}
Each intersection is represented by two incidence facts: for example,
\verb|coll A B X| and \verb|coll D E X| say that $X$ lies on both lines.
A production statement must also include the appropriate distinctness and
nonparallel guards so these are genuine, unique finite intersections.

\hypertarget{cca:formalize-pascal}{}
\subsection*{Concept check \ref{cc:formalize-pascal}: Formalizing Pascal}

Because three noncollinear points determine a unique circle, the three
\verb|cyclic| facts below place $D,E,F$ on the circle through $A,B,C$:
\begin{lstlisting}
Set_Up: ncoll A B C;
        cyclic A B C D; cyclic A B C E; cyclic A B C F;
        coll C D X; coll F A X;
        coll A B Y; coll D E Y;
        coll E F Z; coll B C Z;
Goal: coll X Y Z
\end{lstlisting}
As for Pappus, a fully checked input also supplies distinctness and
nonparallel guards for every intended finite intersection. The
\verb|ncoll A B C| guard matters here: without it, $A,B,C$ would not determine
a unique common circle.
